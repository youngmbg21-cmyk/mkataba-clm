Three Ways The Card Opens

A change card should stop growing downwards inside a 320-pixel column and open into a window big enough to read the reason and the notes in. Three ways to do that, drawn to scale.

12 Aug 2026 Design only · nothing built Same change shown in all three

What is wrong with opening downwards

Today the card is shut, and pressing its header unfolds the rest in place: who filed it, why they asked, the reviewer's note, and the notes-and-reply box, all stacked into a column about 320 pixels wide. Three things go wrong at once.

  • The column jumps. One card opening pushes every card below it down the page. If you were comparing two changes, the second one has moved.
  • The reading matter is squeezed into the narrowest space on the screen. A three-sentence reason and a thread of replies are exactly the content that needs width, and they are getting the least of it.
  • You scroll the whole column to read one card. There is no way to stay put and read down through a single change.

All three options below fix the same thing: the card stays a compact row in the column, and pressing it opens a proper window with its own scroll. What differs is where that window sits — and that choice decides what you can still see while you read.

One rule none of them may break

Accept, Reject and Edit are the commonest presses on this page, and today they sit on the card whether it is open or shut — deliberately, after the last redesign. Wherever the reading matter goes, the verbs must stay reachable without opening anything. In all three drawings the shut card keeps its own action bar, and the window carries a second copy for when you are already in it.

What it gets right

Nothing else on the page moves — the column holds still, the document stays fully readable, and the layer plainly belongs to the card it points at. The smallest change to how the page already feels.

What it costs

The least room of the three, and it has to flip or slide when the card sits near the top or bottom of the window — the fiddliest of the three to get right at every height.

Watch for

Cards low in a long column. The layer must never open half off-screen, and it must move with the column if the reader scrolls.

So what makes it pop out?

A correction to an earlier draft of this page, because it matters. Pressing a card today does not simply unfold it — it does two jobs at once:

  • it takes you to that clause in the document, lighting it and scrolling the paper to it;
  • and it expands the card downwards.

There is also a small chevron in the corner of every card which does only the second one — deliberately, without moving the document. The reason is written into the code: somebody tidying a column is not asking to be taken anywhere.

That is the whole answer. There are already two controls with two meanings, and the fix is to separate the jobs rather than add a third.

ControlTodayProposed
Press the cardGoes to the clause and unfolds the cardGoes to the clause. Only that.
Press the chevronUnfolds the card, document stays putPops the change out into the window

Nothing new is invented. The chevron already exists, already means there is more here, and already refuses to drag the document. It stops being the fold and becomes the door. And the card press stops doing double duty — which is the reason the column jumps today when all somebody wanted was to look at a clause.

Two things must change with it, or it misleads

  • The mark. A downward chevron promises something unfolds downwards. Opening a floating window needs the two-corner expand mark instead, and a tooltip saying what it opens.
  • The size. It is about nine pixels today, which was fair when it was a secondary affordance beside a card that also opened. As the only door to the reasoning it is too small to find with a mouse and far too small for a thumb.

The two alternatives, honestly

Press does both

Navigate and pop out on one press — closest to today, nothing to learn. But the window has to float over the document (the column is against the right edge; there is nowhere else for it to go), so it covers the clause the same press just jumped to. The two jobs fight. I would not.

A "Why" button in the row

The most obvious of the three: read rather than recognised. It costs a fourth button in a row deliberately kept compact beside Accept, Reject and Edit. The right fallback if the chevron is judged too quiet.

What the separation buys

Because the pop-out leaves the paper readable, it can light the clause behind it without scrolling it away — so the argument and the clause are on screen together. That is the whole reason this option beat the centred sheet.

All three become the same thing on a phone

There is no room for a centred sheet, a docked panel or a floating layer on a 390-pixel screen — and there is no need to choose, because the phone already has the right shape for this: a sheet that rises from the bottom and scrolls inside itself. Whichever option wins on the desktop, the phone gets this.

Why this is the same decision, not a fourth option

The phone renders the same card the desktop does — it is not a separate build. So whatever the card's press does, it does on the phone too. The sheet is simply what that press looks like when the screen is one column wide.

It also matters that the counterparty's page uses the same card. Whichever option is chosen, the other side gets it, and their page has no sidebar to dock a panel to — which is a point in favour of A and C, and something B has to answer for.

What I would build, and why

Option C, the anchored pop-out — with one borrowing from B.

You asked for the card to pop out rather than expand down, and C is literally that: the same press, the same card, the same mental model, but the panel floats out over the document instead of shoving the column around. Crucially it is the only one that leaves the clause itself readable while you read the argument about it, and on a negotiation screen that comparison is the work. A scrim over the contract (Option A) makes the biggest window and takes away the thing the window is about.

The borrowing from B is the ‹ › stepper in the header — change 1 of 3 — so a queue of eight can be walked without eight closes. That is B's real advantage and it costs almost nothing to carry across.

If it turns out people want more room than C gives — long reasons, long threads — the honest upgrade is B, not A, and C's panel is the same content in a different frame, so moving is cheap.

The one thing I would not do

Keep both the old downward expansion and a new pop-out. Two ways to open one card is two behaviours to keep in step across four screens — the workbench, the contract tab, the phone and the counterparty's page — and the fold is the thing being replaced, not supplemented.

What I need decided before building

  1. Which option?My recommendation is C with B's stepper. A is the safest to build and the worst to use; B is the best for a long session and the most work.
  2. Is the chevron the door, or do you want a word?See So what makes it pop out? above. My answer: the chevron becomes the pop-out, grown and re-marked, and the card press goes back to doing one job — taking you to the clause. A "Why" button in the action row is the fallback if the mark is too quiet.
  3. Should the verbs sit in the window as well as on the card?I have drawn both. Duplicating them means the reader can decide without leaving; not duplicating them means one place to press and no risk of the two disagreeing.
  4. Does this reach the counterparty's page in the same change?They render the same card, so it will unless deliberately held back. I would let it — their page is the one with the least room and the most need to read a reason properly.
