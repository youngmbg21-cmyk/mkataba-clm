The Contract Atlas

Three ways to draw the contract graph as nodes and links — but as HaTi's own nodes, on HaTi's own links, laid out so that where a node sits is a fact you can read off it. Same capability as the graph you have; none of the borrowed look. Sample data throughout — the seeded demo book.

Insights / The Dial

The Dial

Still a node graph — just not a borrowed one

You were right to want to stay with nodes. The problem was never the shape; it was that the shape you have is the one a physics library hands you for free. A force-directed graph lets a simulation decide where every node lands, so two contracts sitting next to each other means nothing at all, and it ends up looking like every other open-source network diagram on the internet. All three maps below keep nodes and links. What changes is what a node looks like, what a link is allowed to mean, and who decides where a node goes.

Every node

A sheet of paper, not a bubble

Square corners, cream stock, a coloured margin bar for the value stream, the same ruby margin rule your redline uses where the wording leaves your standard, and a dot when a promise is late. A network library draws circles. HaTi draws documents. That alone makes a screenshot unmistakable.

Every link

Three kinds, each a fact on the record

Solid green is family — an amendment hanging off its master. Amber dashed is the payment chain — this one cannot happen until that one does. Faint dotted is the same counterparty. Nothing else is ever drawn, so a line on this graph is always something you could look up.

Every position

Placed by a fact, never by physics

On The Dial, distance from the middle is time and the sector is the value stream. On The Lattice, the column is how far down a chain you are. In The Cabinet, the drawer is the grouping you asked for. Move a node and you would be telling a lie, so nothing drifts.

The three, and what each is for

  1. The Dial — the whole book on one face. Rings are the day each agreement next forces a decision; the six sectors are your value streams. The real links arc across the middle. Click any sheet and the dial re-centres on it, showing just that agreement and everything tied to it. Closest to the graph you have today, and the one that shows the most at once.
  2. The Lattice — what stands on what. Columns are dependency depth, so a master agreement is on the left and everything hanging off it steps to the right. Right-angle connectors, like a wiring diagram, not springs. This is the answer to “if this one goes, what goes with it?” — and it makes a four-deep supply chain visible in a way a hairball never could.
  3. The Cabinet — your book filed the way you ask. Every grouping the graph has today, but the group is a drawn drawer with its contracts filed neatly inside, instead of an invisible gravity well. Links arc between drawers. This is the safest one to ship: nothing is lost, the picture just stops being a blob.

What stays the same

Grouping, filtering, hover-to-focus, click-to-explain, the Copilot box that can re-cut the graph, and the detail rail — all of it still applies, and the rail still answers the one genuinely useful question the current graph asks: what else moves if this ends. Every figure on these maps is already read somewhere in HaTi: the notice period and renewal window from the renewal card, value converted the way the dashboard converts it, the family and payment-chain links from the record itself, late promises from the obligations worklist. The maps borrow; they do not compute, and nothing here invents a score. In this prototype the doors in the rail are drawn but inert.
