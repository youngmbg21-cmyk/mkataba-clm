Why this is not the graph you have now

The Insights tab today draws a force-directed network: bubbles connected by lines, arranged by a physics simulation. It is the shape every open-source graph library gives you out of the box, and that is exactly the problem — it is recognisable as someone else’s. It is also weak information design, because the simulation decides where each bubble lands. Two contracts sitting next to each other means nothing at all.

The one rule

Position has to mean something

In all three maps, where an agreement sits on the screen is a fact you can read off it. Left is sooner. Higher is bigger. A thicker ribbon is more money. Nothing is decided by a physics engine.

The signature

HaTi draws paper, not bubbles

Every agreement on every map is a small sheet: square corners, cream paper, a coloured margin bar for its value stream, and the same ruby margin rule the redline uses where the wording leaves your standard. Nobody else’s chart looks like this, because nobody else’s product is about documents.

The honesty

Absences are said out loud

Contracts with no expiry recorded get their own column rather than being quietly dropped. Money with no exchange rate on file is counted and named, never guessed. Nothing here invents a risk score.

What each map is for

  1. The Horizon answers “what bites next, and what does it cost me?” It places every agreement by the day it next forces a decision and by what it is worth. The sharp idea: the date that matters is not the expiry, it is the last day you can still serve notice. The amber whisker after each sheet is the window between those two dates — the time you actually have. Drag the line to look ahead to any date.
  2. The Flow answers “who pays whom, and where am I concentrated?” Customers on the left, your value streams down the middle, suppliers on the right, ribbon width by value on paper. It makes one thing obvious in a second: on this book, money comes in on one stream and goes out on five.
  3. The Grain answers “where does my paper leave my own standard?” Your playbook across the top, your agreements down the side, one mark per cell. Read a column to find a term you keep conceding; read a row to find the contract you would regret. No competitor can copy this one — it needs your playbook.

How it would work in HaTi

The Atlas replaces the Contract graph tab. Nothing new is stored and no new figure is calculated: every number on these three maps is already read somewhere in HaTi — the renewal window and notice period from the renewal card, value converted the way the dashboard converts it, the customer-or-supplier side from the payment terms page, the standards marks from the playbook review, late promises from the obligations worklist. The maps borrow; they do not compute. Picking any sheet opens the same detail rail, which keeps the one genuinely useful thing the old graph had: what else moves if this agreement ends. Every door in that rail goes to a screen that already exists. In this prototype the doors are drawn but inert.
