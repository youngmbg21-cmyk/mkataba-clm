i

Who I was

Four real jobs, four different reasons to open a contract system. Everything that follows was written from one of these chairs, not from a feature list.

Ama

Owner · coffee exporter, nine staff · Nairobi

“I am the sales team, the finance team and the legal team. A contract arrives as an attachment on my phone at nine at night. If it takes more than a few minutes I will just sign it.”

  • Do not make me open a laptop to file something
  • Tell me what I have promised this month
  • Never let a renewal pass me by

Daniel

Operations manager · logistics, 140 staff · Gothenburg

“I do not own the law, I own the queue. Everybody asks me where their contract is and I do not know, so I ask somebody else and they do not know either.”

  • Nothing sitting on a desk unowned
  • An honest date I can repeat to a customer
  • Numbers that prove this function pays for itself

Priya

Procurement manager · FMCG · 400 supplier agreements

“The signature is the easy part. The deal is the rebate table, the price-change clause and whether their food safety certificate is still valid. None of that is in a contract system.”

  • The commercial terms in one place, traceable to the wording
  • Money we have earned and not claimed
  • Suppliers’ certificates current, without me chasing

Tomás

In-house counsel · one lawyer for 300 people

“I am the bottleneck and everybody knows it. Half of what reaches me should never have reached me. And when the board asks what we are exposed to, I count by hand.”

  • The routine ones must clear without me
  • One live view of what we are exposed to

ii

The house rules I kept to

HaTi already has strong opinions about how things are allowed to appear on a screen. Every proposal below obeys them — which is also the main defence against over-engineering.

No new bannersNot one of these is a coloured strip across a page. Each lands as a row in a list, a column in a table, a card in a column that already exists, or a tab.
The contract never loses a pixelNothing here adds furniture above the wording. Where a screen shows the agreement, the agreement starts exactly where it starts today.
One door per actionWhere a proposal touches something HaTi can already do, it improves that door rather than adding a second one beside it.
An absence is stated, never guessedWhere the wording says nothing, the screen says nothing was recorded. No invented scores, no confidence percentages dressed as facts.
“Sent” means sentAnything that leaves the building reports honestly whether it left, including the new notices and chases.
Borrow, do not rebuildEleven of the fifteen are new views over readings HaTi already makes. Four need genuinely new machinery, and they say so.

1

Getting the paper in

HaTi is excellent once a contract is inside it. Both of these are about the minutes before that, which is where most small companies quietly give up.

S1New

The Mailroom — forward a contract and it files itself

Loudest for Ama, useful to everyone

Every workspace gets its own email address. Forward the attachment from your phone and HaTi reads the file, works out who the other side is, runs the same arrival readings it runs on an upload, and puts it in a short tray called Unfiled. You press File it or you press Not a contract. That is the whole job.

HaTi can already read a dropped file beautifully, and it can already bring in a whole back catalogue in one sitting. What it cannot do is receive one. Today, getting a contract in means being at a computer, finding the file, and choosing a value stream — at the exact moment a busy owner is least willing to do any of it.

The email itself is useful: the sender becomes a suggested contact, the subject line becomes a suggested name, and the message body is kept as the covering note. Nothing is filed without a person pressing something, so nothing appears in the register that nobody has looked at.

S2Extends Key terms

The commercial terms card — read the deal, not just the dates

Loudest for Priya, valuable to Daniel

HaTi reads a contract for its dates, its value, its governing law and its payment terms. In a trading business the deal is somewhere else entirely: the rebate table, the volume tiers, the minimum order, the listing fee, the mechanism by which the other side may raise the price. A card in the Key terms column reads those out of the wording, each one pointing at the clause it came from.

A procurement manager opens a supplier agreement in HaTi and sees an expiry date and a number. The negotiation was about none of that. To find the rebate threshold they open the Word file and use the search box — which is exactly what they did before they bought a contract system.

The rule stays HaTi’s own: where the wording says nothing, the card says not recorded rather than guessing a market-standard figure. Every value carries its clause, so a disagreement is settled by pressing the pill and reading the sentence.

2

Moving work without a lawyer in the loop

Tomás is one lawyer for three hundred people and Daniel owns a queue he cannot see into. These three are about the same thing from two ends: routine work clearing itself, and everyone knowing where the rest of it is.

S3Extends the sign check

Clearance lanes — the routine ones clear on rules, not on a hunch

Loudest for Tomás and Daniel

An administrator writes a lane: our own NDA template, no money, nothing outside the playbook’s green band, counterparty not on the watch list. Anything that satisfies every condition clears straight to signature. Everything else takes the ordinary road. When a contract clears, the screen names the lane and lists the conditions that were actually tested, with the time.

HaTi already runs the check that would decide this — it reads the standards, the obligations and the record before a signature and lists what is holding. What it will not do is act on a clean result. A perfect NDA and a disastrous one both wait for the same human.

This is the one proposal where I would resist the market. Several systems now let a model decide whether a contract is acceptable. HaTi should not: a lane is a rule a person wrote, and the record shows which rule fired. That is defensible to an auditor a year later, which a model’s opinion is not.

S4Extends Requests

A front door with a clock

Loudest for Tomás and Daniel

HaTi already lets anybody in the company raise a request rather than emailing the lawyer. What a request does not carry is the four things that make a queue work: who owns it, how urgent it is, when it is promised, and what is still needed from the person who asked. Add those, sort by the clock, and the queue becomes a worklist instead of an inbox.

A request today is a title, a paragraph and a status. There is no owner, no date and no priority, so the queue is ordered by whoever shouted most recently — which is the exact problem the requests feature was built to solve.

Priority should be worked out, not typed: a request naming a counterparty already on file, on our own template, under the value where approvals start, is routine. One on the other side’s paper with no counterparty on file is not. The clock is a promise the team sets per kind of work, and the screen shows the median it actually achieves beside it.

S5New

“Where is my contract?” — a tracker for the person who asked

Loudest for Daniel, quietly loved by everyone

The person who raised the request gets a link. It shows five steps, which one the contract is on, who is holding it, and the honest date. No sign-in, nothing they can change, nothing commercially sensitive — the same discipline HaTi already applies to what the counterparty may see.

A salesperson who asks for a contract has no way to find out what happened to it except asking a human, who asks another human. That chase is the single most common piece of work a contract team does, and it produces nothing.

It also makes an unpleasant truth visible in a useful way. When the bar sits on With the other side for eleven days, nobody blames the lawyer — and HaTi can say when it last chased them.

3

Holding the other side to the deal

HaTi is very good at telling you a date is coming. All three of these are about the step after that: doing the thing, sending the thing, and proving you sent it.

S6Extends the renewal card

The Notice Desk — draft it, serve it, prove it

Loudest for Ama, Priya and Tomás

A contract that renews unless you object is only dangerous for about three weeks a year. HaTi already knows when those weeks are and what the clause requires. What it does not do is help you write the letter, send it to the address the contract names, and keep proof. The Notice Desk does those three things, for the four notices that matter: do not renew, we are renewing, we object to this price rise, we are terminating.

HaTi tells you a decision is due and quotes the notice period from the wording. Then it stops. The most consequential email a company sends all year is written by hand, in Outlook, to an address somebody remembered. If it goes to the wrong address, or two days late, the contract renews for a year.

The proof is the part the market gets lazy about. A served notice should sit on the trail with the exact words, the address it went to, the time it left, and what the provider said about delivery — using the same honesty HaTi already applies to every other message.

S7Extends obligations

What you are owed — rebates, credits and price holds as money

Loudest for Priya

Once the commercial terms are read (S2), the ones with money in them become entitlements you can track: a rebate earned but not claimed, a service credit due, a price hold about to lapse. They ride the obligations engine that already exists — the same bands, the same chase, the same reminders — with one new state: claimed.

HaTi’s obligations are duties: things somebody must do by a date. The other half of a commercial agreement is money that falls due because a threshold was crossed, and no screen in the product counts it. In practice that money is never claimed, which is why suppliers write the clauses so willingly.

The honesty rule matters more here than anywhere: HaTi reads agreements, not your bank. It can say what the contract entitles you to on the volumes you enter; it cannot say what was invoiced. The screen says so, plainly, and the arithmetic stays where a person can see it.

S8Extends obligations

Certificates that expire

Loudest for Priya, close behind for Tomás

Contracts require the other side to hold things: product liability insurance, a food safety certificate, an organic certification, a tax residency certificate, data processing terms. Each has an expiry date and none of them is the contract. HaTi should hold the document, watch the date, and chase the supplier automatically — using the chase it already has.

Nothing in HaTi holds a supplier’s certificate. In an FMCG business this is the compliance wall: an expired food safety certificate is a stop-shipping event, and it is currently tracked on a spreadsheet by one person who is on holiday in August.

4

Seeing the whole book

HaTi has five Insights tabs and a weekly review, and they are good. These four are the questions the four seats actually asked that none of them answers yet.

S9Extends Copilot

Ask your book — with citations you can open, and questions that keep watching

Loudest for Tomás and Priya

Copilot can already search the wording and answer about the portfolio. Two things are missing and both are small. First, every sentence in the answer should carry the clause it came from, as a pill you press to land on the wording. Second, a question worth asking once is worth keeping: save it, and HaTi re-asks it whenever a contract is filed or signed, and tells you when the answer changes.

You can ask Copilot a good question today and get a good answer. Then you close the tab and the answer rots. Three weeks later somebody signs a contract that changes it and nobody finds out.

A saved question is the cheapest early-warning system a contract system can have, and it costs almost nothing because HaTi already turns an answer into a filtered worklist.

S10Extends the weekly review

The exposure register — one live page for what could hurt you

Loudest for Tomás, read by Ama before she talks to a bank

HaTi’s weekly review already counts uncapped liability and open-ended price clauses. Those counts belong on a live page instead of in a document: one row per kind of exposure, the number of contracts, the money on paper behind them, and a door into the list. A sixth Insights tab, next to Payment terms.

The facts exist but they are trapped in a weekly document, as sentences, with no way to press them. When the board asks what we are exposed to, the answer is assembled by hand — and the person assembling it has no idea which contracts nobody has read.

The last row is the most valuable one and the one every competitor hides: contracts nobody has read. An honest exposure page says how much of the book it cannot speak for.

S11Extends the clause ladder

What the negotiation was worth

Loudest for Daniel and Priya

HaTi already records every position on a clause, in order, with the figures. That is everything needed to say what a negotiation actually moved: payment terms opened at 30 and settled at 45; the liability cap came down from twelve months’ fees to six; the automatic renewal came out. Print that at the end of the negotiation, and add it up across the quarter.

The ladder holds the whole story of a negotiation and nothing ever totals it. A team that negotiates well has no way to show that it does, which is why contract functions get cut first.

One caution, and it should be printed on the screen: converting fifteen days of payment terms into a money figure is a rule of thumb, not a measurement. Say the rule, show the days, let the reader decide.

Acting on many at once

Everything in HaTi happens to one contract. Twice a year something has to happen to two hundred of them, and today that means two hundred afternoons. Both of these arrive through one new door.

S13New

“Do this to these 46” — papering a change across the book

Loudest for Tomás and Priya

Narrow the Contracts page the way you already can — supplier agreements, in force, on our paper — and a single act appears above the table: Do this to these 46. Behind it, two things: send the same amendment to all of them, or ask all of them for a document. HaTi mints one amendment per contract from a template, sends them in waves so a mistake costs ten emails rather than four hundred, and keeps a board of who has signed.

A company name changes, a law changes, an insurer raises a requirement, and a price index moves. Each of those is a week of copying and pasting, and the usual outcome is that half the book never gets updated — which is exactly what an acquirer or a regulator eventually finds.

The discipline that keeps this from being dangerous is the wave. Nothing is sent to everybody at once, and every wave can be stopped. The first wave is the one you choose yourself.

S14New

The diligence pack — the whole book, evidenced, in one press

Loudest for Ama, then Daniel

A bank, an investor, an acquirer or an auditor asks the same question in the same shape: give us every agreement, tell us which ones we cannot assign, which ones die on a change of control, which ones lock you in, and which ones you cannot find. HaTi can answer all five from what it already holds. One press builds the pack: an index, the flags, and the documents.

HaTi can export contracts and it builds a fine evidence pack for a single signature. It has no answer to “send us your contracts and tell us what is in them”, which for a small company is the most stressful week of its year.

The line that makes the pack trustworthy is the one that admits what is missing. Four contracts with no signed copy on file are four questions you will be asked anyway — better to be the one who raised them.

Meeting people where they already are

S15New

Everything HaTi knows is inside HaTi. The person who most needs the renewal warning is the least likely to log in. Email helps, but an email asking for a decision usually becomes a task for later, and later is after the notice window closes.

What I left out, on purpose

These are all things a world-class CLM is sold with. Each one would make HaTi worse, and the reason is usually one of HaTi’s own rules.

A risk score out of 100Every competitor has one and none of them can explain it. HaTi’s standing rule is that an absence is stated and never guessed — a made-up number breaks it, and a lawyer will not defend a figure they cannot derive. The exposure register counts instead, and every count opens.
A drag-and-drop workflow builderIt is the classic enterprise upsell and it is how a product loses its opinions. HaTi’s strength is that there is one way to do each thing; a builder gives every customer four, then support carries the difference for ever.
A separate supplier portalHaTi already has a page the other side can open, with careful rules about what may leave the building. A second front door would drift from the first, and two screens disagreeing about what the product does is the most expensive fault this codebase has.
Another dashboardHome answers “what needs me” and Insights answers “what is true”. A third place to look is a third place for the numbers to disagree. Each proposal above lands in one of the two that exist.
AI that signs, or that accepts their redline for youHaTi freezes wording at the first signature and records how each signature was proven. Letting a model act inside that boundary would throw away the most valuable thing the product has built. Clearance lanes are the safe version: a rule a person wrote, named on the record when it fires.
“How your terms compare to the market”Benchmark data sells well and cannot be checked. HaTi already has the honest version — what you have settled for before, counted from your own book, which is the number that actually helps in a negotiation.
A native phone appHaTi already draws itself properly on a phone, and S15 puts the decisions in the app people are genuinely in all day. An app store release is months of work to reach the same place.
Accounting and ERP integrationTempting for S7, and it is the right answer eventually. But most of the value is in knowing what the paper entitles you to — which nobody currently knows — and that needs no integration at all. Build the ledger first; connect it when a customer asks twice.
Translating the contract itselfHaTi will translate the product into another language but never the agreement, and that is the correct instinct. A translated clause that has not been agreed is not the clause.

The through-line

Almost nothing here is artificial intelligence that HaTi does not already have. Eleven of the fifteen are new views over readings it makes today, and the four genuinely new pieces — an inbox, a notice, a campaign, a chat channel — are plumbing, not cleverness.

What the four seats were actually missing was the step after the insight: HaTi tells you the renewal is coming and does not help you serve the notice; it reads the contract and does not read the deal; it counts the uncapped liabilities in a weekly document you cannot press. Closing that half-step, fifteen times, is what separates a very good contract system from one a procurement manager would fight to keep.
