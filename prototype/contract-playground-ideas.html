The Curious Contract

Five ways to let someone look at a contract instead of only being told what they owe on it — what each one would show, where it would sit in HaTi, and which two are worth building first.

Is it a good idea?

Yes — and it is the biggest thing missing from HaTi. Every screen in the product today answers the same question: what do I owe? Blockers, holds, counts, rows waiting on you. Not one screen answers what am I actually looking at? A person who opens a forty-page agreement they did not write gets a wall of words and a list of chores.

The good news is that HaTi already knows the answer. It records twenty-six terms per contract, can name what each clause is for, remembers every negotiating move the company has ever made, holds a position per clause, and reads every clause into plain English. None of that is drawn as a picture anywhere. So this is mostly a looking problem, not a knowing problem — which is why it is cheap.

One correction, and it matters. Do not build a playground as a place. HaTi built a beautiful read-only overview page for Templates and deleted it three days later — it had forty-four things you could press and not one of them did anything. A page you visit once out of interest is a page nobody opens twice. Build these as layers on the contract you are already reading, and as one fact that every existing screen can borrow. Then curiosity sits on the path to the work instead of beside it.

The real risk is not cost, it is tone. A lawyer's job is to be certain. A product that says “huh, interesting” about a clause can read as a product that is not sure. So every mark in every idea below rests on something checkable — a word count, a recorded term, your own playbook, your own signed history — and never on a hunch. Curiosity is allowed to point. It is not allowed to have opinions.

And one upside worth naming out loud. Almost everything HaTi does well is invisible: the walls, the one funnel, the review gate, the things that quietly refuse to go wrong. None of it demos. A picture of a contract demos in ten seconds.

01Shape

The X-ray

A map of the document down the side of the document. What it is made of, how big each part is, and where something is worth a second look.

Build first

The Document tab already has a switch with two positions: the contract, and the plain English reading beside it. This is a third position on the same switch. Press it and the right-hand column becomes a strip of bands — one band per clause, as tall as the clause is long, coloured by what the clause is for. Press a band and the paper scrolls to it.

The point is proportion and absence. You can see in one glance that the liability clause is the shortest thing in the document and the definitions are the longest — and that there is no force majeure clause at all. Absence is the most interesting thing a map can show, and HaTi has no surface anywhere that shows it today.

Where it lives
A third position on the Document tab's existing view switch. No new page, no new tab, no band across the top.
Built from

Costs the paper
Nothing. It takes the right-hand column the way plain English already does — the contract does not move a pixel.
Watch out for
“Expected, not found” needs a short, defensible list per contract type, or it becomes HaTi inventing a standard nobody wrote down. Start with six clauses that are genuinely in almost every agreement.
02Comparison

Is this normal for us?

Not “does this break the rule” — HaTi already answers that. “Have we ever signed anything like this before?”

Build first

Today HaTi has exactly one idea of unusual: a difference from the playbook — from what the company says it wants. It has no idea of a difference from itself — from what the company actually signs. Those are different things, and the second one is the one that makes a person curious.

The arithmetic already exists in a small corner of the product: the Our Standards page can look across signed contracts, find the commonest value for a term, and say how often it appears. Point that same counting at all twenty-six recorded terms and you get a reading for any contract: where does this one sit against our own book?

The honest half is what makes it trustworthy. Below three signed contracts it refuses to have an opinion — and that refusal is itself the most interesting line on the page: “this is the first one of these you have done.”

Where it lives
Everywhere at once, because it is a fact, not a screen: a small mark on the Overview cell, a row on the list before signing, the amber dots on the X-ray, a line in the brief.
Built from
The counting already written in stdDraftFromSigned, pointed at all of META_FIELDS instead of four. One new file, no route, no stored field, no Copilot spend.
Why first
It is the engine underneath idea 1 and idea 3. Build it once and three screens get cleverer without being redesigned.
Watch out for
“Unusual” must never read as “wrong”. An exclusive deal you chose on purpose is unusual and correct. The wording has to stay flat: you usually sign 30, never this is too long.
03Consequence

The what-if bench

The playground proper: pull a lever, watch the consequence, commit nothing. The only one of the five where the reader touches the contract.

Build — on a short leash

This is what most people mean by a playground. Drag payment terms from 60 days to 30 and watch what it does to the cash tied up in the deal. Push the notice period out and watch the date you must act by move. Accept their liability position and watch the exposure register gain a row.

HaTi has the machinery: it already knows how to write a figure into a clause properly (“thirty (30) days”, in words and digits, without disturbing anything around it), and the negotiation ladder already draws a scale per clause with your standard, your fallback and their last position on it.

The leash is the whole design. This product's first rule is that it never invents a number. So the bench may only move things the record can answer — days, dates, cash timing, and where the book's average lands. It may not price risk, score a deal, or tell you what a cap is worth. And nothing it shows reaches the contract until the reader presses the same Apply button they would have pressed anyway.

Move the slider. Nothing on this bench touches the record — to change the contract you still press Apply in the clause editor, the one door that has always filed a change.

Where it lives
A tab in the Copilot rail beside the clause editor, next to the ladder and the figure scale that are already there. Not a page of its own.
Built from
ladderTrack for the scale, ladderWriteFigure for writing the number into the wording, payTermsData for the book's average, fxHome for money, renewalWindow for dates.
The one hard rule
It files nothing. Every route out of the bench goes through the clause editor's existing Apply. A second way to file a change is the most expensive mistake this codebase knows how to make.
Watch out for
Scope creep into a deal scorer. The moment it multiplies a risk by a probability it has stopped being HaTi.
04Sequence

The first sixty seconds

Not a map — a walk. Seven stops through a document that just arrived, one clause at a time, each with a sentence saying why it is a stop.

Build later

When a document arrives, HaTi already reads it: it writes a brief, checks it against the playbook, finds the obligations and fills what it can. Today that arrives as four small tiles and a paragraph. The reader is still left alone with forty pages.

Instead, pace it. Seven stops. Each one scrolls the paper to a clause, puts the plain English beside it, and says in one line why this clause was chosen — it moves money, your playbook disagrees with it, it is unusual for you, or it creates a promise somebody has to keep. Then next.

The ranking is free — it is the same set of marks the X-ray already computes, sorted. So idea 4 costs almost nothing once idea 1 exists, which is exactly why it should wait for it.

Arrived from Nordkalk AB · 2 hours ago Stop 3 of 7
Unusual for you 44 of your 47 signed agreements cap liability. This one does not.
11. Liability
Neither Party excludes liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence. Save as aforesaid, the Supplier's liability under this Agreement shall be unlimited in respect of any claim arising from defective Goods.
In plain English
If the goods are faulty, there is no ceiling on what the supplier can be made to pay. Most of your agreements put a ceiling on this.

Press a stop. The walk is a press, never an interruption — it never opens by itself and it can be closed for good.

Where it lives
A button on the arrival strip that already appears on a new contract's Overview: Walk me through it.
Built from
The arrival reading that already runs (triageRun), plus the X-ray's marks, sorted. The plain English column already exists per clause.
Why later
It is the cheapest of the five after idea 1, and close to pointless before it. Building it first would mean writing the ranking twice.
Watch out for
Tone. A lawyer being walked through their own document can feel talked down to. Keep the sentences factual and let anyone skip to the end in one press.
05Story

How this deal moved

Every argument HaTi has ever hosted is on the record and nothing has ever drawn it. A replay of the negotiation, clause by clause, round by round.

Cheap — and it is the demo

HaTi stores the whole argument: who asked for what, on which clause, in which round, and how it ended. The deal board shows where things stand now. Nothing shows how they got there.

One track per argued clause, one column per round, a dot for every move coloured by who made it. You can see at a glance that exclusivity settled in round two and liability is still open in round four — and, from your own history with this counterparty, that this is two rounds longer than usual.

Of the five this is the least useful per hour of work and by far the most likeable. It is the screen someone shows a colleague. It also quietly answers a question no CLM answers: where do we always give in?

Where it lives
A reading on the contract's own History tab, and a second home on the deal board where the same clauses are already listed.
Built from
ladderRungs per clause, ladderBoard for the ordering, and precedentMine for “how long this usually takes with them” — which already accepts a counterparty.
Nothing new
No route, no stored field, no model. It is drawing something the record has held all along.
Watch out for
Do not let it grow verbs. The moment a dot can be pressed to reopen a settled change it becomes a second way to decide things, and decisions belong on the redline column.

The one rule that keeps all five safe

Every one of these shows things the reader can already act on somewhere else in HaTi. That is the danger and the discipline: none of them may grow its own way to change the contract. A band on the X-ray scrolls the paper. A dot on the replay opens the clause. The bench hands its wording to the Apply button that has always filed changes. They are windows, not doors.

Keep that line and curiosity is free. Cross it and you have built a second product that disagrees with the first one — which, by this codebase's own account, is the most expensive mistake it knows how to make.
