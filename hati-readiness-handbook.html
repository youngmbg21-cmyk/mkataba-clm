Bottom line, before the detail

HaTi is a genuinely well-built application sitting on a fragile operational foundation.

The product is stronger than most things at this stage. The logic that decides who may see, edit, send and sign a contract is enforced on the server, not just hidden in the interface, and it is covered by 3,020 automated tests across 195 files — many of them deliberately hostile, testing what happens when a dishonest browser lies to the server. I ran the suite while writing this: all 3,020 pass. The security document in the repository is more candid about its own limitations than most funded startups' are. That is real engineering, and you should not undersell it.

The operations around it are where a due-diligence meeting would get uncomfortable. There is no automated backup. There is no monitoring, so if HaTi went down at 2am you would find out from a customer. There is no staging environment, so every change is tested in production. The database is a single file on a single 1 GB disk on a single server, unencrypted at rest. And the system is single-tenant by design — the code's own notes say it plainly — so you cannot put two customers on one installation without a real risk of one seeing the other's contracts.

None of these are hard problems. Most are a day or a week of work each. But they are the exact list a buyer's IT reviewer works through, and today most of the answers are "no".

Part one

The vocabulary

Roughly a dozen words carry most of a technical conversation. Once these are solid, you can follow almost any discussion about a web platform.

The three pieces every platform has

The restaurant

Think of a restaurant. The dining room is what the customer sees and touches — the menu, the table, the waiter taking the order. The kitchen is where the actual work happens, out of sight, and where all the rules live: what can be cooked, who is allowed to order what, whether you've paid. The store room holds the ingredients permanently, so that closing for the night doesn't lose everything.

Dining room = frontend. Kitchen = backend. Store room = database.

The frontend is everything that runs inside the user's own web browser: the buttons, the layout, the contract text on screen, the animations. It is downloaded to their computer and runs there. This matters enormously for security: anything in the frontend, the user can read and change. A determined person can open their browser's developer tools and rewrite it. So the frontend can never be where a rule is enforced — it is only where a rule is displayed.

The backend is a program running on a computer you control, somewhere else. The browser sends it messages ("give me contract 47", "save this change") and it decides whether to obey. Because the user cannot touch it, this is the only place a rule is genuinely enforced. When an engineer says "we enforce that server-side", they mean the real lock is on the kitchen door, not just a sign in the dining room.

The database is organised, permanent storage. Not a folder of files — a structured system that can answer questions quickly ("every contract expiring next month, worth over 5 million, in the Procurement stream") and that guarantees a half-finished write doesn't corrupt everything.

The rest of the words

An API (Application Programming Interface) is the fixed menu of messages the backend will accept. Instead of the frontend rummaging around in the kitchen, it can only place orders from a defined list: GET /api/contracts means "list the contracts", POST /api/shares means "create a share link". The API is the shape of the kitchen door. A well-designed API is narrow — few doors, each carefully guarded.

A framework is a pre-built skeleton for an application: it makes most of the structural decisions for you, and you fill in the rest. A library is smaller — a specific tool you call when you need it. The blunt distinction: your code calls a library; a framework calls your code. A framework saves months but locks in someone else's assumptions; a library saves hours and stays out of your way.

A dependency is any code written by someone else that your platform needs in order to run. This is normal and universal — nobody writes everything from scratch. But every dependency is a company or a volunteer you are now trusting: if they ship a bug, you ship a bug; if their account gets hacked and malicious code is published, it can end up running on your server. This is called supply chain risk, and it is one of the questions an enterprise buyer will ask.

A repository ("repo") is the project — all its files, plus its complete history. See Part 2.

An environment is one running copy of the whole system. Professional teams keep at least three: development (on an engineer's own laptop, breakable), staging (a full copy that looks exactly like the real thing, for final checks), and production (the real one, with real customers' real contracts in it). The reason for staging is simple and expensive to learn the hard way: you want to discover a mistake on fake data.

A build is the step that converts what engineers write into what actually runs — compressing files, converting newer language features into ones older browsers understand, bundling hundreds of files into a few. A deploy is the act of putting the new version onto the production server so real users get it.

What a programming language is, and how one gets chosen

A programming language is a precise way of writing instructions that a computer can carry out with no ambiguity. Languages differ in the same way that a legal contract differs from a memo: some are extremely strict and verbose, catching errors before anything runs; others are loose and quick, catching errors only when they happen.

In practice, a language is almost never chosen on technical merit alone. The four real reasons are:

  • Who you can hire. A brilliant language nobody in your city writes is a liability.
  • What already exists. If mature, well-tested libraries for your problem exist in one language, that usually settles it.
  • What the team already knows. Speed of building beats theoretical elegance at early stage, every time.
  • Where it has to run. Anything running inside a web browser must ultimately be JavaScript, because that is the only language browsers execute.

What HaTi is actually built with

Found in the repository
  • Language: JavaScript, on both sides. The frontend is roughly 66,000 lines across about 55 files in js/. The backend is a single file, server/server.js, of 8,111 lines.
  • Backend runtime: Node.js, version 22.5 or newer.
  • Backend framework: Express 4 — and it is the only production dependency in the entire project.
  • Database: SQLite, using the version built into Node itself (node:sqlite) rather than an add-on. About 26 tables — contracts, users, sessions, shares, signatures, templates, an AI usage log, and more.
  • Frontend framework: none. No React, no Vue. Plain JavaScript modules loaded by the browser directly.
  • Build step: none. The files you write are the files that run.
  • Styling: Tailwind CSS, but vendored — copied into index.html rather than fetched from the internet at page load. There are no external scripts or fonts loaded from third-party servers. Fonts are self-hosted in fonts/.

What this choice means in practice — the honest version, both directions:

The good. One production dependency is remarkable restraint. A typical Node application of this size pulls in 800–1,500 third-party packages, each one a small trust decision and a potential vulnerability. HaTi has one. No external CDN scripts means no third-party can push code into your users' browsers and no outage elsewhere takes your app down. No build step means what an engineer reads is exactly what runs — no class of bugs where the build produces something different from the source. One language across both sides means one skill to hire for. These are real, defensible engineering decisions, and you should say so confidently.

The cost. An 8,111-line single backend file and a 306,000-character single frontend file (js/core.js) are past the size where one person can hold them in their head. This is the kind of thing that makes a technical reviewer wince, and makes onboarding a second engineer slow and risky. SQLite is a superb database, but it is a file on one machine — it cannot be shared by several servers at once, which caps how you can grow (Part 3). And JavaScript's looseness means a whole category of mistakes that a stricter language would catch before running are only caught by tests — which is presumably why there are 195 test files.

Worth knowing

You will hear "monolith" used as an insult. It isn't one. A monolith is one program that does everything, and for a product at HaTi's stage it is almost always the correct choice — it is simpler to run, cheaper to host, and easier to reason about than the "microservices" alternative. The problem with HaTi is not that it is a monolith; it is that individual files inside it have grown too large.

Part two

Where code lives

Three places at once — your laptop, Git, and GitHub — and understanding why is the difference between a hobby project and a company asset.

The three places, and what each one is actually for

Your computer is the workshop. It is where files get edited and where the app runs while you are building it. It is fast, private, and completely disposable — nothing important should exist only here.

Git is a system that records the history of a project. It lives on your computer, inside a hidden folder in the project. Every time you deliberately record a snapshot — a commit — Git stores exactly what every file looked like at that moment, who did it, when, and a short note about why. It never overwrites; it accumulates. This means you can go back to any past state, and you can see precisely what changed between any two moments.

The contract analogy

Git is version control for code, and you already know version control — it is what an executed contract with a full amendment history gives you. A commit is an executed amendment: dated, attributed, describing what changed and why, and never erased. "What did clause 12 say in March, and who changed it?" is a question Git answers in one second, forever.

GitHub is a company that hosts Git repositories on the internet. It is not the same thing as Git — Git is the technology, GitHub is a service built around it. Three reasons professionals use it:

  • It is the off-site copy. Your laptop can be stolen, dropped, or encrypted by ransomware.
  • It is where more than one person can work. Everyone has their own full copy; GitHub is the agreed meeting point.
  • It is where the safety machinery runs. Automated tests, code review, deployment triggers — all hang off GitHub.

So the answer to "why not just pick one?" is that they do different jobs. Your computer is where work happens; Git is the memory; GitHub is the safe, the meeting room, and the alarm system.

Branches, commits, pull requests, review

A commit is one recorded change with a message. Good practice is small and frequent: each commit does one thing and its message says what and why. This is what makes it possible, months later, to find the exact change that broke something.

A branch is a parallel line of history. The official one is called main. When you start a piece of work you make a branch off main, work on it freely — including breaking things — and main stays untouched and shippable the whole time.

A pull request (PR) is the request to fold your branch back into main. It shows exactly what changed, line by line. Two things get attached to it: automated tests that must pass, and a human who must approve. Code review is that human step, and its value is not mainly catching bugs — it is that a second person now understands that part of the system. In a one-person company that sounds pointless; it is what makes the company survivable when there are two people, and it is what an acquirer looks for.

Branch protection is the setting on GitHub that makes this real rather than aspirational: it forbids anyone — including you — from writing directly to main, and refuses a merge until tests pass. Without it, the whole process is a habit that gets skipped at 11pm.

What happens if your laptop dies

If your work is committed and pushed to GitHub: you buy a new laptop, run one command to clone the repository, and you have every file and every commit back. You lose only what you hadn't pushed.

If it is not pushed: it is gone. There is no recovery. This is why "the truth lives on GitHub" is the operating rule — your laptop holds a working copy, not the asset. If it isn't on GitHub, it doesn't exist.

Important distinction

GitHub protects your code. It does not protect your customers' data — the contracts, users and signatures live in the database on the production server, which GitHub never sees. Those need a separate backup, and that is a different problem, covered in Part 4.

How HaTi is set up today

Found in the repository
  • Git is in use, hosted on GitHub at youngmbg21-cmyk/mkataba-clm. There is a main branch and feature branches.
  • Secrets are handled correctly: .env files are excluded from Git, and a placeholder-only .env.example is committed so a new engineer knows what to fill in. The Render deployment file explicitly refuses to store the email API key. This is textbook and you should keep doing it.
  • Automated tests run on GitHub on every push and every pull request (.github/workflows/tests.yml). This is the single most valuable piece of professional infrastructure in the project. Running the suite while writing this gave 3,020 tests across 660 groups, all passing, in under four minutes.
  • The visible history is 62 commits by 2 authors, with the earliest dated 9 August 2026 — three days before this assessment.
  • package-lock.json is deliberately excluded from Git, and the workflow comment says so explicitly. CI therefore runs npm install rather than npm ci.
  • There is no CODEOWNERS file, no Dependabot configuration, and no LICENSE file.

Three things about that are worth being direct about.

The missing lockfile is a genuine problem, not a style preference. A lockfile records the exact version of every dependency, down to the digit. With one, a build today and a build in six months produce byte-for-byte the same thing. Without one, the server installs "whatever is newest that roughly fits" at the moment it happens to deploy — so a dependency author can change your production system without you doing anything. HaTi's exposure is unusually small because it has one production dependency, but the same command also installs the test tooling, and "we can't reproduce a build from six months ago" is a bad answer in a security questionnaire. This is a five-minute fix: delete two lines from .gitignore, commit the lockfile, change npm install to npm ci in the workflow.

Three days of history and 62 commits does not match a codebase this size. A 75,000-line application is many months of work. So the history was almost certainly reset, squashed, or the project was re-created at some point. That is not a disaster, but it does mean you have lost the ability to answer "when did this change and why" for everything before 9 August — the single most useful thing Git gives you.

Part three

Hosting, explained properly

Whose computer is it, where does it physically sit, who pays for it, and what breaks when it stops.

What hosting actually is

Strip away the language and hosting is this: a computer, owned by someone else, sitting in a warehouse, that is switched on permanently and connected to the internet with a permanent address. You rent a share of it by the month. That warehouse is a data centre — a building full of racks of computers with industrial power, backup generators, cooling, and physical security.

That rented computer is what people mean by "a server". The word describes a job, not a special kind of machine: a server is any computer whose job is to sit and wait for requests and answer them. Your laptop could do it; it just isn't switched on permanently, doesn't have a fixed address, and would go down when you close the lid.

"The cloud" means only that you are renting rather than owning — you never see the machine and can rent more of it in a minute. It is not vague. It is someone's warehouse.

Domain, DNS, and HTTPS

A domain name is a name you rent annually from a registrar — hati.co.ke, say. You rent it; you never own it. Letting it lapse is one of the classic ways a company loses its product overnight.

DNS (Domain Name System) is the internet's phone book. Computers find each other by number (an IP address like 216.24.57.1), not by name. DNS is the lookup that turns your name into that number. When someone types your address, their computer asks DNS "where is this?", gets a number back, and connects. When engineers say "it's a DNS issue", they mean the phone book entry is wrong or hasn't spread yet — changes can take minutes to hours to reach everyone.

HTTPS is the encrypted version of the web's basic protocol, and it does two separate jobs. It scrambles everything travelling between the browser and your server, so anyone watching the network — an airport wifi operator, an internet provider — sees unreadable noise instead of contract text. And it proves identity: an SSL/TLS certificate, issued by an authority browsers already trust, confirms the server really is who it claims to be, which is what stops someone impersonating your site. The padlock in the browser is the visible result. For a contract platform HTTPS is not optional; it is the floor.

Why frontend and backend are often hosted separately

They have genuinely different needs. The frontend is a fixed set of files that never change between users — everyone downloads the same thing. That can be copied to dozens of machines around the world so it loads instantly everywhere, and it is extremely cheap, often free. The backend is a running program that must think, check permissions and read a database. It needs real computing power, it costs proper money, and it must be near the database.

So the common professional pattern is: frontend on a global distribution network (Netlify, Vercel, Cloudflare Pages), backend on an application host (Render, Fly.io, AWS, a rented server), database managed separately.

It is entirely legitimate to serve both from one program instead. It is simpler, it removes a whole class of configuration problems, and at HaTi's scale the performance difference is irrelevant. HaTi does exactly this, and it is the right call for now.

Where the database sits, and whose problem it is

Two models, and the difference matters more than almost anything else in this document.

Self-managed: the database is a file or program on your own server. You are responsible for backups, for upgrades, for making sure it doesn't fill the disk, for recovery when it corrupts. It is cheap and completely under your control.

Managed: you rent a database as a service (Amazon RDS, Supabase, Neon, Render Postgres). The provider handles backups, replication, patching and point-in-time recovery. It costs more and you have less control. Almost every company selling software to other businesses uses managed, for one reason: a provider whose entire business is not losing databases will lose yours less often than you will.

How HaTi is hosted today, and what it costs you in risk

Found in the repository
  • render.yaml defines a deployment to Render: one web service, plan: starter, running npm start — a single Node process serving both the API and the frontend.
  • A 1 GB persistent disk is mounted at /var/data, and HATI_DATA points the app at it. The file's own comment warns that without this, every deploy wipes all contracts and accounts.
  • The database is a single SQLite file, hati.db, on that disk. Uploaded documents are stored in the same database.
  • A health check is configured at /api/status, so Render can tell whether the app is alive.
  • HTTPS is handled by Render; the app sets HTTPS=true, which switches on secure cookies, HSTS and an http→https redirect.
  • DEPLOYMENT.md also documents self-hosting behind Caddy or nginx with a systemd service — a genuinely good, complete runbook.
  • Only one service is defined. There is no staging environment.

The risks this creates, in order of how much they should worry you:

  • Everything lives in one place, with no automatic copy. The database, the uploaded documents and the audit trails are all on one 1 GB disk. The documented backup is "an admin clicks export" or "stop the process and copy the file". If that disk is lost, corrupted, or if someone deletes the wrong thing, your customers' executed contracts are gone. For a contract platform this is the single most serious finding in this document.
  • You cannot run a second copy. SQLite is a file — two servers cannot safely share it. The code confirms this from another angle: the security document notes that rate limiting is held in memory and "would need a shared store (e.g. Redis)" for multiple instances. So you cannot scale out, and you cannot do a zero-downtime deploy. Every update is a brief outage.
  • 1 GB is smaller than it sounds, because uploaded PDFs and DOCX files are stored in the database. A few hundred scanned contracts will approach it. The failure mode when a disk fills is ugly and abrupt.
  • Deploying is testing. With no staging environment, the first time a change runs against real data is when customers are using it.
  • Data residency is undefined. Render's default regions are in the US and Europe. If HaTi is sold in Kenya under the Data Protection Act 2019, where the data physically rests is a question you will be asked, and today the answer depends on a dropdown someone picked. The repository's own security note says the MVP assumes the customer self-hosts, which sidesteps this — but the Render deployment does not.

Part four

What "robust" means for a contract platform

Ten questions a buyer's security team will ask. For each: why it matters, and what bad, adequate and professional look like.

A contract platform is held to a higher standard than most software, for three reasons: the documents are legally operative, they are commercially sensitive, and they belong to someone else. Losing a customer's photos is bad; losing the executed contract they are relying on in a dispute is existential — for them and for you.

1. Backups, and whether you can actually restore

Why it matters: everything else on this list protects you from an attacker. Backups protect you from yourself — a bad deploy, a wrong command, a corrupted file. Most data loss is self-inflicted.

The critical distinction, and the one that catches people: a backup you have never restored from is not a backup. It is a hope. Backups fail silently and routinely — the job stops running, the file is truncated, the format changed, the encryption key is on the machine that died. The only proof is a restore drill.

BadAdequateProfessional
Manual, occasional, on the same machine as the original — or none. Automatic daily copy to different storage in a different location, retained for 30 days. The above, plus point-in-time recovery, plus a restore drill performed on a schedule with the recovery time written down, plus backups encrypted with keys held separately.

HaTi: the mechanisms exist and are well documented — a full workspace ZIP export via the admin screen or GET /api/export/workspace.zip, and copying hati.db. Both require a human to remember. There is no scheduled job, nothing writes off the machine, and there is no evidence of a restore ever having been tested. Rating: Gap.

2. Encryption in transit

Why it matters: without it, contract text travels the internet as readable text that anyone on the path can capture.

BadAdequateProfessional
Plain HTTP, or HTTPS available but not enforced. HTTPS everywhere, valid certificate, http redirected to https. The above, plus HSTS so browsers refuse to try plain http at all, modern TLS only, and automatic certificate renewal that has been observed to work.

HaTi: handled properly. With HTTPS=true set — and it is, in render.yaml — the app issues Secure cookies, sends HSTS, and 301-redirects plain http. The one caveat is that this is configuration-dependent: a deployment where someone forgets the flag silently loses all three. Rating: Strong.

3. Encryption at rest

Why it matters: this is about what happens when someone gets hold of the storage itself rather than the running system — a stolen backup file, a decommissioned disk, a misconfigured cloud bucket, an attacker who reaches the filesystem. If the data is encrypted at rest, they have noise. If not, they can read every contract in a text editor.

BadAdequateProfessional
Plain files on an unencrypted disk. Anyone with the file has the data. Full-disk encryption at the host level (most cloud providers do this by default), so a physically removed disk is useless. The above, plus encryption at the database or field level for the most sensitive columns, with keys in a dedicated key-management service that rotates them — so even a compromised application server cannot read everything.

HaTi: the database file is plain SQLite with no application-level encryption — I searched, and the only matches for "encrypt" in the server are code that detects password-protected PDFs. So it rests on whatever Render's disk encryption provides, which is real but which you have not verified or documented, and which does not protect a backup ZIP downloaded to a laptop. Rating: Missing at the application level.

4. Authentication — proving who someone is

Why it matters: every other control assumes the system knows who is asking. Two technical points worth understanding, because you will be asked about both. Passwords must never be stored as text, nor as a simple hash — they must be salted (a unique random value per user, so identical passwords don't look identical) and slow-hashed with an algorithm deliberately designed to be expensive, so guessing millions of candidates is impractical. scrypt, bcrypt and argon2 are the accepted choices; MD5 and plain SHA-256 are not.

BadAdequateProfessional
Passwords stored plainly or with a fast hash; sessions that never expire; no lockout on repeated guesses. Salted slow hash, server-side sessions in HttpOnly cookies, expiry, rate limiting on login, self-serve reset with single-use expiring tokens. The above, plus mandatory two-factor authentication for administrators, single sign-on for enterprise customers, session listing and revocation, and alerts on suspicious sign-in.

HaTi: the fundamentals are right. Passwords use scryptSync with a per-user salt. Sessions are server-side, HttpOnly, SameSite, Secure under HTTPS, expire after 30 days, rotate on login, and can be listed and revoked from settings. Login is rate-limited to 10 attempts per 15 minutes. Counterparties verify by emailed one-time code before a signature is accepted. What is missing is two-factor authentication for your own admins — and an admin account on a contract platform is a very high-value target. Rating: Adequate.

5. Access control — what a known person may do

Why it matters: in a contract system the same document must be visible to some colleagues and invisible to others. The professional standard is that the check happens on the server, on every single request, and that the interface merely reflects a decision made elsewhere. The classic failure is hiding a button while leaving the door open.

BadAdequateProfessional
Permissions enforced in the browser. Anyone who edits their own page gets in. Server-side role checks on every route that changes data. The above, plus row-level filtering applied before data is serialised, denial by default, tested adversarially against raw requests, and identifiers that reveal nothing on refusal.

HaTi: this is the strongest part of the entire system, and it is genuinely above benchmark. Roles are enforced server-side for every mutating route. Folder-level ("value stream") restrictions are resolved from settings on every request and applied as a SQL filter before anything is serialised — across the contract list, single fetch, search, statistics, exports, and the AI prompts. A request for a contract outside your scope returns 404, not 403, so a refusal doesn't confirm the record exists. There is a separate per-member right controlling whether monetary values are visible, and the server strips those fields from responses rather than hiding them in the browser. Executed contracts are immutable server-side — the sealed wording, negotiation record and audit trail are restored from storage on any attempt to modify them. And all of it is tested against raw requests, not just through the UI. Rating: Strong.

6. Tenant isolation

Why it matters: if two customers share one installation, one query written without a customer filter shows Company A's contracts to Company B. There is no recovering from that commercially. It is the defining risk of multi-customer software.

BadAdequateProfessional
Shared database, customer filtering applied inconsistently or in the browser. Every query scoped to the tenant, enforced centrally so it cannot be forgotten, and tested. The above, plus database-enforced row-level security or a database per customer, plus automated tests that specifically attempt cross-tenant access.

HaTi: the repository is admirably honest here. MULTITENANCY-NOTES.md states the status as single-tenant: an org_id column exists on contracts and users but defaults to one workspace, and the queries are not scoped by it — safe today only because there is exactly one tenant. Its own recommendation is not to enable multi-org signup until that work is done, and to run one process per customer in the meantime. That is the correct call, and it is a genuinely defensible architecture — but it means every customer needs their own installation, with its own cost, its own deploy, its own backup. Rating: Gap (as an architecture; Missing if you intend to sell shared hosting).

7. Audit trails

Why it matters: for a contract platform the audit trail is not an operational convenience, it is part of the product's legal value. "Who changed clause 12, when, and what did it say before?" must be answerable years later, and the answer must be tamper-evident.

BadAdequateProfessional
Records overwritten in place; no history of who did what. Append-only log of significant actions with actor, timestamp and before/after. The above, plus cryptographic hashing so alteration is detectable, exportable evidence packs, and an audit trail that cannot be edited even by an administrator.

HaTi: strong, and clearly built by someone who understood why it matters. Every contract carries an audit array. On signature the fully rendered text is frozen and hashed with SHA-256, and signed contracts always render from that frozen copy, so what is displayed provably equals what was sealed. Each signature records the signer, the capacity they signed in, the method, timestamp, user agent and server-side IP. A downloadable evidence pack and printable certificate accompany every executed contract. Every AI Copilot exchange is separately logged to its own table. Divergence between live and sealed wording is reported, never repaired — which is exactly right. Rating: Strong.

8. Uptime and monitoring

Why it matters: the question is not whether you will have an outage — you will. It is whether you find out from your monitoring or from an angry customer. These are different companies.

Three distinct things, often confused: monitoring watches whether the system is up and how it is performing; error tracking captures the details of individual failures so you can fix them; alerting is what wakes a human.

BadAdequateProfessional
Nothing. You learn about outages from customers. An external uptime check every minute that emails or texts you, plus an error-tracking service capturing crashes with enough context to debug. The above, plus dashboards for response time and error rate, alerts on degradation not just death, a public status page, and a written on-call process.

HaTi: there is a health endpoint at /api/status and Render is configured to use it, which means Render will restart a dead process. Beyond that there is nothing: no error-tracking service, no metrics, and 18 console statements in an 8,111-line server. If HaTi threw an error for one specific customer this afternoon, you would not know, and you would have no way to find out what happened. Rating: Missing.

9. Disaster recovery

Why it matters: backups are the ingredient; disaster recovery is the recipe. Two numbers define it, and enterprise buyers ask for both by name. RTO (Recovery Time Objective): how long until we are running again. RPO (Recovery Point Objective): how much recent work we accept losing. "Four hours and one hour" is a real answer; "we'd sort it out" is not.

BadAdequateProfessional
No plan. Recovery would be improvised by one person. A written runbook, tested at least once, with stated RTO and RPO. The above, rehearsed on a schedule, with infrastructure defined as code so the environment can be rebuilt from the repository, and a documented failover region.

HaTi: half of this exists and is better than average — DEPLOYMENT.md is a real runbook with restore instructions, and render.yaml means the server itself can be rebuilt from the repository in minutes. What is absent is the data half: no off-site copy to restore from, no tested drill, no stated RTO or RPO. Rating: Gap.

10. Secrets management

Why it matters: a "secret" is any credential the system needs — API keys, database passwords, signing keys. The classic catastrophe is committing one to Git, where it lives in the history forever and gets scraped by bots within minutes of the repository becoming public.

BadAdequateProfessional
Keys written into the code or committed to Git. Keys supplied as environment variables, excluded from Git, set in the host's dashboard. The above, plus a dedicated secrets manager, rotation on a schedule, per-environment separation, automated scanning that blocks a commit containing a key, and an audit of who accessed which secret.

HaTi: the hygiene is genuinely good. .env and .env.* are excluded from Git; a placeholder-only .env.example is committed as documentation; render.yaml marks the email key sync: false with a comment saying it must never be committed. Two soft spots: an admin can paste an Anthropic API key into the settings screen, where it is stored in the database — which is unencrypted at rest — and the security document records that in static/local mode the AI key sits in the browser, noting this is scheduled for retirement. Rating: Adequate.

11. Data residency and data protection

Why it matters: Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 imposes real obligations on anyone processing personal data, including registration with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner, a lawful basis for processing, defined retention, and a route for individuals to access or delete their data. If you host on behalf of customers you are a data processor and they will require a Data Processing Agreement before signing.

HaTi: the security document addresses this head-on and lists what is outstanding: ODPC registration, a DPA for any hosted offering, documented retention and deletion, and a subject-access path beyond the admin backup. It also notes the MVP assumes customer self-hosting, which places residency with them. That is an honest and workable position for a pilot — it is not a position you can hold once you host anyone yourself. Rating: Gap.

What must be fixed before your first paying customer

These are ordered. Doing them in this order means that at every point you have made the biggest remaining reduction in risk.

  1. 1Automated off-site backups, with one tested restore 2–3 daysA scheduled job that copies the database and files to separate storage daily, retained 30 days, encrypted. Then delete nothing, but restore a copy into a fresh environment and confirm the contracts are there. Write down how long it took. Until this is done, one bad afternoon destroys the company.
  2. 2Monitoring and error tracking 1 dayAn external uptime check hitting /api/status every minute and alerting your phone, plus an error-tracking service capturing unhandled failures. This is mostly configuration, and it converts "we don't know" into "we know" for a low monthly cost.
  3. 3Decide and document the tenancy model 2–5 daysTake the repository's own advice and run one installation per customer. Script it so provisioning a new customer is one command, not an afternoon of clicking, and so each has its own backup. Do not relax the single-workspace restriction until the scoping work in the notes is finished and tested.
  4. 4A staging environment 1 dayA second Render service from the same blueprint, pointed at the staging branch, with fake data. Every change goes there first. This is the cheapest quality improvement available to you.
  5. 5Close the source-control gaps Half a dayCommit the lockfile and switch CI to npm ci. Turn on branch protection for main. Make CI run every suite the project defines, not three of thirteen. Turn on GitHub secret scanning.
  6. 6Confirm and document encryption at rest 1–2 daysVerify in writing that Render's disk is encrypted; if it is not, or you cannot confirm it, move the database to a managed encrypted store. Separately, ensure backup files are encrypted before they leave the machine — that is the copy most likely to end up somewhere careless.
  7. 7Two-factor authentication for administrators 3–4 daysAdmin accounts can see every contract in the workspace. A stolen admin password should not be sufficient on its own.
  8. 8The data-protection paperwork 1–2 weeks, mostly legalODPC registration where applicable, a Data Processing Agreement your customers can sign, a written retention and deletion policy, and a named contact. What to do next

A list you can hand to a developer without translating it, and the five questions to ask anyone you hire.

The brief, ready to hand over

Copy from here

The items below are written for an engineer. They assume familiarity with the repository and require no decisions from me beyond the ones already stated.

  1. 1Commit package-lock.json; switch the workflow to npm ci.Remove the lockfile entry from .gitignore. This makes builds reproducible.
  2. 2Make CI run npm run test:all, not three suites.The project defines thirteen; .github/workflows/tests.yml runs test, test:browser and test:selection. Split them into parallel jobs if the run gets slow.
  3. 3Enable branch protection on main: no direct pushes, tests must pass, one approving review.Also switch on GitHub secret scanning and push protection.
  4. 4Build a nightly backup job.Copy $HATI_DATA/hati.db (using SQLite's own backup command, not a plain file copy, so it is consistent) plus any file storage to off-site object storage. Encrypt before upload. Retain 30 days. Alert on failure — a silent backup job is the standard failure mode.
  5. 5Perform and document a restore drill.Restore last night's backup into a fresh environment, confirm contract count and that an executed contract still verifies against its hash. Record the elapsed time as the RTO.
  6. 6Add uptime monitoring and error tracking.External check on /api/status every minute with phone alerting; an error-tracking SDK capturing unhandled exceptions and failed requests, with contract text and personal data scrubbed before sending.
  7. 7Create a staging service from render.yaml with its own disk and seeded fake data.Point it at a staging branch. Nothing reaches production without passing through it.
  8. 8Confirm in writing whether the Render disk is encrypted at rest, and what its snapshot policy is.If the answer is unsatisfactory, plan the move to a managed encrypted database.
  9. 9Script per-customer provisioning.One command that creates a new isolated instance with its own HATI_DATA, its own backup schedule and its own monitoring entry, per MULTITENANCY-NOTES.md.
  10. 10Add two-factor authentication for the Admin role.Time-based one-time codes are sufficient. The one-time-code machinery already exists for counterparty signing and can be reused.

Five questions to ask any engineer or vendor

Ask these in this order. What you are testing is not whether they know the answer — it is whether they have lived the answer. Specifics mean experience; generalities mean they have read about it.

  1. "When did you last restore a production system from a backup, and how long did it take?"The single most revealing question you can ask. Anyone who has run something real has a story with a number in it. "We have backups" is a non-answer — everyone has backups; the question is whether they work.
  2. "Walk me through what happens between you finishing a change and a customer seeing it."You are listening for tests, a review, a staging environment, and a rollback plan. If the honest answer is "I push it", that is workable in a prototype and disqualifying for a contract platform.
  3. "How would you find out that the system was broken for one customer this afternoon?"A good answer names monitoring, error tracking and logs. A weak answer describes checking manually. If the answer is "they'd tell us", you have found your next hire's first task.
  4. "If someone got a copy of our database file, what could they read?"This tests whether they think about defence in depth or only about the front door. The right answer for HaTi today is uncomfortable — everything — and a good engineer will say so plainly rather than reassure you.
  5. "What in this codebase would you not want to be responsible for, and why?"The most useful question of the five. Anyone who says "nothing, it all looks fine" has either not looked or is telling you what you want to hear. You want the person who names the 8,000-line server file, or the missing backups, within thirty seconds.

A closing note on proportion

It would be easy to read Part 6 and conclude that HaTi is in bad shape. That is not the conclusion. The hard part of a contract platform — the part that takes years and that most competitors get wrong — is the correctness of the negotiation, permission and sealing logic, and HaTi's is genuinely good: enforced in the right place, tested adversarially, and documented honestly by people who understood why each rule existed.

What is missing is the operational shell around it: backups, monitoring, staging, and the paperwork. That work is unglamorous, largely a matter of configuration, and mostly measured in days rather than months. It is also the work that separates a product that impresses in a demo from one a company will trust with its executed contracts.

Do the nine items in Part 6 first, in that order. Then sell with confidence — because at that point the honest answers to the security questionnaire will be good ones.
