HaTi — two contract negotiations, re-run on the code as it stands today

A user-experience review of the HaTi contract platform (repo youngmbg21-cmyk/mkataba-clm), based on reading the current source at commit b1a333f, 26 July 2026. Every claim was checked against the real code, and the file and function names are given so you can find them. This replaces an earlier review of the older code — the platform has changed a great deal since, so most of the old findings no longer apply, and there are new ones that do.

Read this first: one thing is silently broken

The "Send updated version" button does not send anything to the counterparty. It updates the shared page and shows Wanjiru the message "Updated version sent to Erik Lindqvist". No email goes out. Erik is never told. The contract's own history then records "Updated version sent to Erik Lindqvist on their existing link" — which is not true.

In plain terms: from round 3 onwards, she thinks the ball is in his court and he does not know there is a ball. This is checked in the code below (reshareToLastRecipient in js/core.js, and the PUT /api/shares/:token/payload route in server/server.js — neither sends mail). It is a small fix, and it is the most important item in this document.

The two people in the story

Wanjiru owns a small catering company in Nairobi. She drafted a 12-month supply agreement inside HaTi from the built-in "Raw Material Supply Agreement" template (js/templates.js, js/wizard.js).

Erik is a procurement manager at a mid-size Swedish firm. In Scenario 1 he will only use Microsoft Word with tracked changes over email. In Scenario 2 he works inside HaTi.

What the app actually has today

The honest inventory, from reading the code — not from what a contract tool usually has:

YES — Export any contract as a real Word .docx file, from the toolbar (js/docxwrite.js).
YES — Upload the counterparty's marked-up Word file onto the same contract; it becomes a numbered round (js/wordflow.js).
YES — Accept or reject each change separately in a round, with a running count (js/versioning.js, diffBlocks).
YES — Headings, clause numbers and layout survive the whole negotiation (richFromTextEdit in js/richdoc.js).
YES — One long-lived link per counterparty that always shows the current wording (server/server.js, durable shares).
YES — A two-way conversation beside the contract, with replies (portalThreadHtml), and a list of points still open.
YES — Signing with an emailed 6-digit code, a tamper-proof seal, and a full history.
NO — Any notification when she presses "Send updated version". See the red box above.
NO — A working Word download on the counterparty's page. It produces the whole contract as one unbroken paragraph (verified — see Friction 2).
NO — Clause-by-clause editing for the counterparty. Erik still edits the entire contract in one plain typing box (js/views/portal.js, line 629).
NO — A way to attach a paper-signed copy to the contract it belongs to. It still becomes a separate record.
NO — A counterparty account. Erik has no login; access is by link only. (Reasonable, but it means everything depends on that link reaching him.)
PARTLY — Signing needs email delivery configured on the server. Without it, Erik cannot get his code and must phone Wanjiru for it.
PARTLY — There are two different Word download buttons on the same screen that behave differently (Friction 3).

Scenario 1 — Erik will only use Word

Wanjiru drafted the agreement in HaTi. Erik exchanges Word files by email. Six rounds.

Round 1 She sends the draft as a Word file

  • She wants to email Erik an editable Word copy.
  • Screen Contract workspace (js/views/contract.js). The toolbar now has a Word button beside PDF.
  • Steps One click. The file downloads with the title as a real heading, the clause numbers intact, bold party names still bold, and the signature block present. I verified this by having HaTi's own Word reader open the file it just wrote: 16 separate lines, clause 5 still numbered 5.
This works, and it is the difference between using the product and abandoning it. The half-hour of copy-paste-and-rebuild that used to start every negotiation is gone.
But there are two Word buttons on this screen. The toolbar Word button just downloads. A second button lower down, "Download .docx for Word review", downloads and freezes online editing until the file comes back. Same screen, same word, different consequences, and nothing explains the difference.
Friction 3. Circled in red: two controls that both say "Word" and do different things (ws-word in js/views/contract.js versus startWordReview in js/wordflow.js).

Round 2 Erik returns his marked-up file

  • She wants to get his edits into this contract and see them highlighted.
  • Screen The Word panel on the contract → "Upload returned .docx".
  • Steps Two clicks and a file picker. HaTi reads his tracked changes, counts them, and files them as Round 1 under his company's name — not hers. The review window opens straight away.
The whole round-trip now closes inside the app. His authorship is preserved in the record, which is the thing that was most wrong before.

Round 3 She decides, change by change

  • She wants to accept his delivery change and refuse his payment change.
  • Screen The review window (reviewProposedRound, js/versioning.js). Each change has its own Accept / Reject, a running count at the bottom, and a reply box.
  • Steps Two clicks plus a short reply. The contract is rebuilt from her decisions and the formatting survives — the title is still a title, clause 5 is still clause 5.
This is now the strongest screen in the product. The old "accept everything then delete the bits you didn't want, by hand, in a plain box" is gone.

Round 4 She counters

  • She wants to send a middle position back to Erik.
  • Screen Edit → change the wording → save. Then the Word button again.
  • Steps About six. The exported file carries the current wording, not the original draft — the app checks for this and regenerates rather than handing over a stale file.

Round 5 Changes arrive by email prose, not as a file

  • She wants to record two changes Erik described in the body of an email.
  • Screen Edit → tick "These changes came from Nordkust Industri AB (received outside HaTi)".
  • Steps Three. It files as a round in his name and waits for her decision, instead of being logged as her own edit.
The history now tells the truth about whose words are whose, even when the conversation happened in email.

Round 6 Agreement — and the one remaining dead end

  • She wants to get the final text signed and sealed.
  • If Erik signs electronically she sends him a single-answer link; he signs with an emailed code; the contract is sealed with a fingerprint and a full history. This path works.
  • If they sign on paper — common for cross-border deals — the scanned copy still cannot be attached to this contract. Uploading it creates a second, unrelated record, and the contract she nursed through six rounds never honestly reaches "Signed".
Still blocked at the last step, for paper signing only. HaTi already understands the idea of a contract "executed outside HaTi" — it uses it for imported historical contracts — but that route is not offered on a contract you negotiated in the app.
Friction 5. Circled: the only way in for a paper-signed copy is a brand-new record.

Scenario 1 verdict

6/10   (the earlier review of the older code scored this 2/10)

The single worst friction point: the two competing "Word" buttons. It is not dangerous, but it is the first thing Wanjiru meets, and one of them silently freezes her ability to edit with no explanation of why or how to undo it. Everything downstream of it now works.

Would a real Kenyan SME owner keep using this? Yes, for this scenario. The loop that used to drive her out of the product — export, receive markup, decide, counter — now runs inside it end to end, and the record of who changed what stays honest. She would grumble at the duplicate buttons and she would hit a wall if the deal is signed on paper, but she would not abandon the tool. That is a genuine change from the previous review, where she gave up around round three.

Scenario 2 — Erik negotiates inside HaTi

An honest correction to the premise, unchanged from before: there is no counterparty account to invite Erik into. "Both parties in HaTi" means he uses the no-login review page (js/views/portal.js) that opens from a link. What has changed is that the link can now stay alive for the whole negotiation.

Access How Erik gets in

  • She does Share → name and email → leaves "Keep this link open for the whole negotiation" ticked → Send. Six steps.
  • He does Clicks the link. No account, no password. He sees the contract, who sent it, and when it expires.
One hidden dependency: if the server has no email provider configured, nothing is actually delivered — the message sits in an admin outbox and Wanjiru must copy the link out herself. The app tells her this, but only in small text after she has pressed Send.

Round 1 Erik proposes changes

  • He wants to change the delivery clause and the payment terms, and explain why.
  • Screen "Propose edits (redline)". The entire contract appears as plain text in one large typing box (pt-redline-text, js/views/portal.js line 629). He scrolls to find clause 4, edits it there, and writes one comment for the whole round.
  • Steps About seven.
This is unchanged and it is now the weakest part of the product. A procurement manager expects to comment on a clause. He is handed the whole agreement as one wall of text and asked to retype parts of it. One stray keystroke deletes a paragraph and nobody notices until the comparison screen. Everything on Wanjiru's side got clause-aware this year; his side did not.
Friction 4. Circled: the single plain typing box that stands in for a contract editor on the counterparty's side.

Round 2 She decides, and refuses one point with a reason

  • Screen An amber "Changes returned" strip on the contract → the review window with a control per change and a reply box.
  • Steps Three clicks and a sentence of reply. She accepts the delivery change, refuses the payment change, and types "Net-30 stands, or a 2% price increase."
Both halves now reach him: the decision and the reason, shown as a conversation beside the contract, with the refused point listed under "Still open between us".

Round 3 She sends the updated version — and the negotiation silently stops

  • She wants to put the revised contract back in front of Erik.
  • Screen "Send updated version" on the negotiation panel. One click. She sees "Updated version sent to Erik Lindqvist".
  • What actually happens The shared page is quietly updated. No email is sent. Erik has no reason to revisit the link. The contract history then records that an updated version was "sent" to him.
This is the worst failure in the product today, and it is invisible to both sides. She has been told it was sent. He was told nothing. Both of them wait. Two days later she chases him by WhatsApp — the exact thing the tool exists to stop — and only then discovers the round never left the building. The false line in the contract's own history is the part that should worry you most: this is a product whose entire case rests on the record being true.
Friction 1 — the critical one. Circled: the message that never leaves. Verified in reshareToLastRecipient (js/core.js) and the PUT /api/shares/:token/payload route (server/server.js): neither sends mail.

Round 4 He does eventually reopen the link

  • Screen The same link as before — it is still live and still answerable, which is a real improvement.
  • What he sees A banner saying the contract has been revised since he last opened it, a one-click comparison of exactly what moved, the conversation so far, and the point that is still open.
  • Steps One click to see the changes.
When he does arrive, the page is genuinely good. That is what makes the missing notification so costly — the destination is right, the invitation is missing.

Round 5 The money round

  • He does "Request changes" → a "propose a different value" field appears → he offers a lower annual figure.
  • She does Accepts. The contract value updates and any internal approval she had already collected is correctly voided, so she must get re-approved before signing. Honest, but an extra loop she will not expect.

Round 6 Acceptance, signature, seal

  • He does "Accept the wording (without signing)" — a genuinely useful distinction — then, on the signing pass, Approve & sign, draws or types a signature, and enters a 6-digit code sent to his email.
  • She does Ticks intent-to-sign and signs. HaTi freezes the exact text, seals it with a fingerprint, and writes the history. The signed document is the properly formatted one.
The email dependency bites hardest here. If the server has no mail provider, Erik cannot receive his code. The page tells him to ask Wanjiru to read it out of her settings screen — which means a Kenyan SME's contract signing depends on a phone call and a code read aloud.
Friction 6. Circled: the point where signing stops if the server was never given an email key.

A bug on Erik's side, found while checking his Word route

The counterparty page offers "Download as Word (.docx)". I ran it: the file it produces contains the entire contract as one single unbroken paragraph — no headings, no clause numbers, no line breaks. The same export from Wanjiru's toolbar produces sixteen properly structured lines. The cause is a mismatch in what the page hands to the writer (it passes plain text but labels it as formatted, so the writer treats the whole contract as one block). There is no test covering this path, which is why it shipped.

Friction 2. Circled: the file Erik actually receives from the counterparty page.

Scenario 2 verdict

5/10   (the earlier review of the older code also scored this 5/10)

The single worst friction point: "Send updated version" does not send anything. A negotiation that silently stalls at round three is worse than one that is merely tedious, because neither party knows it has stopped — and the contract's own history claims otherwise.

Why the score did not move despite real improvements. Deciding a round change-by-change is now excellent. Formatting survives. The conversation and the open points are visible to both sides. The link stays alive. Every one of those is a genuine gain. But they are all gains on the owner's side of the glass, and the two things standing between Erik and a smooth negotiation — a plain typing box instead of a document, and a message that never arrives — are both still there. A better product that stops working at round three does not score higher than a clumsier one that limps to the end.

Would a real Kenyan SME owner keep using this? Conditionally. For a one- or two-round deal she signs and moves on, and the experience is good. For a contested six-round negotiation she will conclude the tool is unreliable — not because it is slow, but because she was told something happened that did not. That is the kind of failure that loses trust permanently, and it is the cheapest thing on this list to fix.

What to fix, in order

Eight items, ordered by pain removed per unit of work. Sizes: small = days, medium = 1–2 weeks, large = several weeks. The mobile/WhatsApp counterparty portal is deliberately not on this list.

1. Make "Send updated version" actually send

Criticalsmall

Change: when the link is refreshed, email the counterparty that the contract has been updated. Only claim it was sent once something has left. If mail is not configured, say so plainly and offer the link to copy — and do not write "sent" into the contract's history.

Why it hurts today: every round after the first silently stalls. She waits, he waits, and the record contains a statement that is untrue. It undermines the one thing this product sells: an honest account of what happened.

2. Fix the Word download on the counterparty's page

small

Change: give the Word writer the formatted document rather than plain text labelled as formatted. Then add a test that exports from the counterparty's page and checks the result has more than one line.

Why it hurts today: Erik asks for a Word copy and receives the contract as one unreadable paragraph. He will assume the tool is broken, and he will be right.

3. One Word button, with the locking as a visible choice

small

Change: keep the toolbar Word button. Replace the second one with a tick-box next to it: "Pause online editing until the file comes back." Explain in one line why she might want that.

Why it hurts today: two buttons say "Word" on the same screen and one of them silently freezes editing. She will hit the freeze without knowing what caused it or how to undo it.

4. Let the counterparty edit clause by clause

large

Change: replace the single typing box on the review page with the document broken into clauses, each separately editable, each with its own comment box. The owner's side already works this way.

Why it hurts today: it is the last place in the product where a contract is treated as a wall of text. It invites accidental deletions, forces one comment to cover several unrelated asks, and it is the counterparty's entire impression of the tool.

5. Attach a paper-signed copy to the contract it belongs to

medium

Change: on a contract that has reached agreement, offer "we signed this on paper — attach the scan". Record it as executed outside HaTi, keep the negotiation history, and store the file's fingerprint.

Why it hurts today: cross-border deals are still signed on paper. Today that scan becomes a second, unrelated record and the six rounds of history are orphaned. HaTi already has this concept for imported contracts; it is simply not offered here.

6. Do not let signing depend on a mail provider

medium

Change: check at share time whether email actually works, and warn her before she sends. Offer a second way to verify the signer that does not require reading a code down the phone.

Why it hurts today: a workspace set up without an email key looks fine until the moment of signature, then fails at the least recoverable point in the whole process.

7. Test the counterparty's own screens

medium

Change: add automated tests that drive the review page the way Erik uses it — download the Word file, propose edits, respond twice on the same link — and check what he actually receives.

Why it hurts today: both bugs in this review are on Erik's side of the glass, and both shipped because the tests only ever exercise Wanjiru's side. The counterparty is half of every contract and none of the test coverage.

8. Show her whether he has actually seen the current wording

medium

Change: on the contract, show plainly: "Erik last opened this on 24 July — before your latest changes." The server already records when a link is opened; nothing surfaces it against the current version.

Why it hurts today: she has no way to tell the difference between "he is thinking about it" and "he never saw it". With item 1 fixed this matters less, but it is what turns a silent wait into a visible state.

Where this leaves the product

The engine is now good. Deciding a redline change by change, keeping a contract's formatting through six rounds, filing the other side's wording under their own name, one link that stays alive — these are the hard parts, and they work. The previous review's central complaint, that the best engineering in the codebase was switched on for the wrong case, no longer holds.

What is left is thinner but sharper: the counterparty's half of the product has not kept up. Erik still edits in a plain box, the Word file he downloads is broken, and the message telling him there is anything to look at is never sent. Items 1, 2 and 7 are days of work between them and would remove every serious failure in this review. Item 4 is the real remaining project.

Method note: this review is based on reading the current source (js/, server/server.js, test/) at commit b1a333f and tracing each action through the functions named above. The two bugs described — the Word download on the counterparty page and the missing notification — were each confirmed by running the real code, not inferred from reading it. Step counts come from the actual click paths. No feature described here was assumed from what a contract tool usually has.
