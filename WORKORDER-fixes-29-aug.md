## 1 · The overview's cards ARE the categories

> *"In templates overview, the cards are supposed to be segmented by library so
> all in image 2."* — and then, when the first answer was wrong:
> *"The cards should represent the categories in the attached so that you have a
> card for all templates and the respective metrics. You have a card for
> standard contracts, a card for warehousing etc."*

**BUILT TWICE, and the first one was wrong.** I read "segmented by library" as
the template cards grouped under library headings. The attachment was the
table's own rail, and what it asks for is a card PER CATEGORY. The picture was
the specification and the sentence was the caption.

The overview now shows one card for each row of that rail: **All templates,
Company standard, Counterparty paper, HaTi standard, Samples**, then one for
**every value stream** — each carrying that category's own metrics (how many
templates, how many contracts have come off them, and the deviation rate across
them), under the rail's own two captions.

- **The counts are the rail's**, checked against it rather than worked out twice.
- **A stream card wears its stream's colour**, a library card wears none —
  which is what the picture draws.
- **Pressing a card opens the table narrowed to that category**, with the rail
  lighting the row it is narrowed to and "All templates" as the way back.
- **A rolled-up rate flattens extremes on purpose**: a library holding one bad
  template and eleven good ones is a middling library. The panel beside the wall
  is still where single templates get named.
- **Every value stream gets a card, even an empty one** — "have we any
  warehousing paper?" is better answered by a card saying none than by an
  absence you have to notice.
- **Nothing is held back any more.** The wall used to show as many template
  cards as fitted the screen with a "see all" under it; a bounded handful of
  category cards always draws in full, so that machinery stands down.

## 2 · The pencil goes to the editor — it was a dead press

> *"we agreed that the panel on the right would go away and when i click on the
> pencil i would go straight to edit with copilot page and that is not
> working."*

Correct, and worse than it looked: pressing the pencil did **nothing at all** —
no page, no panel, no error message. The pencil had been given the editor's name
to call, and nothing on the contract was listening for that name.

- Wired now, beside the change row's own door onto the same page.
- The pencil also stops claiming the editor on a screen that has not loaded it,
  so it can never again offer a door with nothing behind it.
- **No test in the suite pressed that pencil**, which is why it shipped: the one
  file that used to press it was re-staged away from it in the same change that
  moved its destination. It presses it for real now, and against yesterday's
  code that check reports the owner's bug verbatim.

## 3 · Click in the words and type

> *"Also, also hate that I have to click on a pencil for me to edit in the edit
> with copilot page. The platform should just allow me to edit without have to
> go through pencil clicks which is annoying from a user experience point of
> view. Let me just edit like I am in google docs but the platform should track
> which clause I am editing."*

On the Edit with Copilot page, clicking in the wording now starts typing, and
clicking in **another** clause's wording moves the page to that clause and
starts typing there. The caret lands where the press did.

- **The pencil stays** — it is the visible sign that a clause can be written on,
  and it is still what turns typing off. It is no longer the toll gate.
- **Yesterday's rule still holds**: arriving on a clause that already carries
  marks still SHOWS those marks. Nothing is hidden from somebody who has just
  put their cursor in a clause, which is why an explicit press outranks it.
- **A press on a button, a tag or a field is never read as a press in the
  words**, and on the two readings that draw no marks ('As agreed', 'With
  changes') a press does nothing at all — typing there would be measured against
  a document the reader is not being shown, which is the rule the pencil and
  Apply already follow.

**ONE THING BEYOND THE ASK, and it is said out loud.** Moving off a clause
throws away wording that has not been filed, and that was silent. Making the
gesture cheap made that loss easy, so **it now asks first** — the same question
the page already asks when you leave it altogether, in the same words ("Leave
this clause?"), and only where something has actually been typed. It applies to
the pencil and the clause dropdown too, because three doors onto one act
answering differently is how a product starts contradicting itself.
