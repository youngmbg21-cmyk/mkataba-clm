ii

Three devices HaTi already has for staying quiet

This is the part that makes the budget above possible, and none of it is new thinking. HaTi already solved “do not overwhelm me” three times, wrote down why, and the fifteen additions simply use the same three devices rather than inventing a fourth.

Device one

Nothing to say, nothing drawn

A card with no content does not draw an empty box — it draws nothing at all, and the column closes up behind it. HaTi decided this after an empty bordered rectangle made a page look broken.

Already doing it: the renewal card. It is not there at all until the contract is inside its 90-day window, so on most contracts that column looks exactly as it always did.

Device two

Offered, not displayed

A control that only some people want lives behind Adapt filters — and then draws itself the moment it is actually narrowing something, so you can always see what is being hidden from you.

Already doing it: the Signed-year filter. Off the bar by default, on the bar the second it is doing work.

Device three

No count at zero, amber only above it

A number beside a door appears only when it is above zero, and only turns amber when something is actually late. A permanent badge stops being read within a week.

Already doing it: the Obligations tab count, the sidebar counts, the alert dot.

And one rule added for this setAn addition must land in a container that already exists: a row in a list, a column in a table, a card in a column, a scope on a table, an act on a card. If a proposal needs a new container, it is the proposal that is wrong.
Which leaves exactly one exceptionThe Exposure tab on Insights. It is new surface, it is admitted as new surface, and it earns it by replacing a count that is currently trapped inside a weekly document nobody can press.

vi

Six releases, one screen each

This is the part that decides whether the product feels like it is improving or like it is churning. Fifteen features shipped one at a time means fifteen separate moments of “something moved again”, scattered across nine screens. Shipped by screen, a person learns one changed screen, once, and then it stays still.

vii

The fifteen in short

What each one actually is, for anyone reading this without the first edition to hand. They came out of four seats: an owner running a nine-person exporter from her phone, an operations manager who owns a queue he cannot see into, an FMCG procurement manager whose deal is the rebate table rather than the signature, and one in-house lawyer serving three hundred people.

S1The Mailroom

Forward a contract to the workspace’s own email address and it files itself, read and ready to confirm. HaTi can send email; it has never been able to receive one.

Borrows: the Word and PDF readers, the arrival readings, the duplicate check.

S2Commercial terms

Reads the rebate, the tiers, the minimum order and the price-change mechanism out of the wording, each pointing at its clause. Today HaTi reads the dates and the value; the deal is elsewhere.

Borrows: the extraction route, the way the renewal card quotes a clause.

S3Clearance lanes

A rule an administrator writes — our template, no money, nothing outside the playbook — that clears routine contracts to signature. The record names the lane and the conditions tested.

Borrows: the pre-signature check, the playbook verdicts, the approval chain.

S4A front door with a clock

Requests gain an owner, a road, and a promised date — and the clock stops while the ball is back with the person who asked.

Borrows: the request record, the worklist shape, the value streams.

S5Where is my contract?

A read-only tracker for whoever raised the request: which step, who is holding it, the honest date. No login, no values, no internal notes.

Borrows: the counterparty page and its rules about what may leave.

S6The Notice Desk

Drafts the non-renewal or termination notice from the clause, sends it to the address the contract names, and keeps proof on the trail.

Borrows: the renewal reading, the email provider’s honest delivery report.

S7What you are owed

Rebates earned, credits due and price holds about to lapse, tracked as obligations with one new state: claimed. The money most companies never go and get.

Borrows: the whole obligations engine, including the amount it already carries.

S8Certificates that expire

Insurance, food safety, tax residency, data processing terms: held, watched, and chased automatically when they lapse.

Borrows: the obligations engine, the chase, the file store, the calendar.

S9Ask your book

Answers carrying the clause each sentence came from, and a question you can keep — re-asked whenever a contract is filed or signed, telling you when the answer moves.

Borrows: the Copilot tools, the search, the answer-to-worklist door.

S10The exposure register

Uncapped liability, open-ended price clauses, traps that renew themselves — live, countable, openable, with the money beside them and the unread contracts admitted.

Borrows: the weekly review’s own counting, the risk scan, the conversion.

S11What it was worth

Opened at 30 days, settled at 45. The ladder already holds every position; nothing totals it. Concessions counted too, or it is a marketing document.

Borrows: the clause ladder and the memo builder.

S12The family check

Does the order form contradict the master agreement? Dates, payment terms, liability, precedence, missing data terms — checked against the family’s own documents.

Borrows: the family links, the clause reader, the pre-signature rows.

S13Do this to these 46

An amendment campaign across a cohort, sent in waves, with a board of who has signed and a stop button.

Borrows: amendments, templates, the round send, the register filters.

S14The diligence pack

The whole book for a bank, an investor or an auditor: an index, the five flags they always ask about, and an honest list of what is missing.

Borrows: the exports, the evidence pack, the conversion, Copilot.

S15HaTi in the chat

The same alerts, arriving in WhatsApp, Slack or Teams. A reply can record a decision. A reply is never a signature.

Borrows: the alerts builder, the approvals, the daily brief.

viii

What we did not add

Half of keeping a product light is the list of things you refused. These are all standard in the category, and each one would have cost surface that the fifteen above deliberately did not spend.

A sixteenth door in the sidebarEvery one of these could have been a nav item — a Mailroom page, a Notices page, a Compliance page, a Campaigns page. That is how contract systems end up with thirty-one doors and a search box to find them. Thirteen doors in, thirteen doors out.
A risk score out of 100Every competitor has one and none can explain it. The exposure register counts instead, and every count opens onto the contracts behind it.
A sixth room tabCommercial terms and required documents both had a good case for being their own tab. They are cards instead, in a column built for cards, because a tab is permanent and a card can be absent.
Another dashboardHome answers “what needs me” and Insights answers “what is true”. A third place to look is a third place for the numbers to disagree.
A drag-and-drop workflow builderThe classic enterprise upsell, and the fastest way for a product to lose its opinions. Clearance lanes are four conditions and a destination, written down and named on the record when they fire.
AI that signs, or accepts their redline for youHaTi freezes wording at the first signature and records how each signature was proven. Nothing in the fifteen reaches inside that boundary.
Market benchmark data“Your terms versus the market” sells well and cannot be checked. HaTi already has the honest version: what you settled for last time, counted from your own book.
A separate supplier portalHaTi already has a page the other side can open, with careful rules about what may leave. A second front door would drift from the first.
Accounting and ERP integrationThe right answer eventually, and not yet. Most of the value in “what you are owed” is knowing what the paper entitles you to — which nobody knows today, and which needs no integration at all.

The through-line

The first edition asked what HaTi was missing. This one asks the harder question, and the answer is that the product already knew: it had written down, three separate times, how to add something without making itself louder — a card that draws nothing when it has nothing to say, a control that hides until it is doing work, a count that does not appear at zero.

Fifteen new abilities, two new things to look at, and no new way of working anything. Every addition is a scope, a card, a row, a column, a switch or an act — controls a person already uses today. The only surface actually spent is one tab on Insights, and it buys back a set of numbers currently trapped in a weekly document nobody can press.
