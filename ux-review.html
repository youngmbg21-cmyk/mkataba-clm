HaTi — two people, one contract, six rounds

What happened when I sat a Nairobi caterer and a Swedish buyer down in front of the software and made them negotiate. Written in plain English. Every technical word is explained in brackets the first time it appears.

How this was done

I ran the real software on a real server and opened two browser windows: one for Wanjiru, one for Erik. Then I clicked the buttons that actually exist, in the order a person would click them, and wrote down every place the software stopped working or stopped making sense.

Nothing here was decided by reading the code. Every problem below was seen happening on a screen, and every fix below was seen working on a screen afterwards.

One honest limit. This test machine could not download the file that gives the app its colours and spacing, so every screen came up unstyled. That means I could check what the software does and what it says — but not how it looks or whether buttons are big enough to tap. Nothing in this review claims otherwise.

The two people

Wanjiru — owner, Karibu Catering, Nairobi

Fourteen staff. She does her own contracts between kitchen shifts. She has never used contract software. If something takes more than a minute she goes back to WhatsApp.

Erik — procurement manager, Nordfrakt AB, Sweden

Signs about forty supplier contracts a year. He has used proper contract software before. He reads clauses, and he will not sign anything where he cannot see what changed.

The score, cycle by cycle

What went wrong, and what was done about it

Round 1  The first message said "sign this", not "let's talk"

What Wanjiru was trying to do: send Erik the first draft so he could comment on it.

What happened: she pressed Share, typed his name and email, and sent. Erik opened the link and the first thing he saw was a green tick and the words "Ready to sign" — on a contract nobody had discussed for one second.

Why: the software worked it out by itself. It reasoned "no changes have been proposed, so there is nothing to argue about, so this must be for signature." On a brand-new draft that is exactly backwards. And nowhere on the Share screen could Wanjiru see or change that decision.

The damage: Erik was never shown the negotiation room at all — the best screen in the whole product. To ask for a change he had to guess between two buttons, neither of which said "negotiate".
Fixed: the Share screen now asks, in words, what the link is for — Negotiate or Sign — and says under each one which screen the other person will land on. The software's own guess now asks "is this deal finished?" instead of "has it started?"

Round 2  Erik asked for a change and there was no way to send it

What Erik was trying to do: push back on the rejection window and the payment terms.

What happened: he pressed the button beside a clause, typed the wording he wanted, and saved. The software did everything right — it numbered the change, stamped it with a fingerprint (a code that proves the wording has not been altered since), and listed it as waiting.

Then he looked for a Send button. There were exactly two buttons on the screen: Decline and Ready to sign. Neither of them sends a proposed change.

The damage: the single most important thing a counterparty does in this whole product could not leave his browser. If he closed the tab, the work was gone. Wanjiru's app never heard of it. This was the worst defect found.
Fixed: the change list now carries a send that counts what he has asked for and says who it is going to — "Send 2 changes you have asked for to Karibu Catering". When it arrives, Wanjiru's copy files each change itself, so the fingerprint is created on the trusted copy rather than taken on faith from a public web page.

Round 2  Nothing on the screen named the one thing you are there to do

The button beside each clause said Edit. Erik does not own this contract and knows it, so a button marked Edit reads as one he is not allowed to press. It is also the wrong word: pressing it does not edit anything — it asks Wanjiru for a change, and she can say no.

Fixed: the button is now called Change, and hovering it says "propose a change to this clause — it goes to the other side to accept or reject". When nothing has been proposed yet, the screen says "press Change on any clause to ask for different wording" instead of describing a list that is still empty.

Round 5  A whole round of work vanished, and nobody was told

What Erik was trying to do: accept Wanjiru's compromise wording, and drop the point she had refused so the deal could move.

What happened: he did both, pressed send, and his screen said it had gone. HaTi's server recorded the message as delivered. Wanjiru's contract showed neither thing. Her history had no entry. And because the server thought it had been delivered, it never tried again. The round was gone.

Why: when the answers arrived, the software wrote them into its memory and then refreshed the screen. The refresh loaded the older saved copy back over the top — a fraction of a second before the new answers were due to be written to disk. So the older copy was what got saved.

The damage: silent loss of a counterparty's work. No error message, no warning, no way for either person to know. This is the kind of thing that ends with two people on the phone accusing each other of not sending something.
Fixed: the answers are now written to disk before anything on the screen is allowed to redraw. Checked afterwards on a full six-round run: the acceptance, the dropped point and the "ready to sign" message all arrive and all survive reloading the page twice.

Round 5  Dropping a point only counted if you pressed the right button

When Wanjiru refuses one of Erik's requests, the deal is stuck until one of them gives way. Erik can drop the request — the polite way to concede — and HaTi is unusual in offering that at all.

But dropping it only actually reached Wanjiru if Erik pressed Ready to sign. If he pressed Send instead, the software threw the withdrawal away. His screen said the point was settled. Her screen went on showing a live disagreement. Neither could see why the deal would not move.

Fixed: a dropped point now counts whichever button sends it.

Round 4  Sending round two gave Erik a second link

There were two ways to send the next round, and they did different things. One quietly updated the link Erik already had open. The other made a brand-new link and left the old one working. Nothing on the screen said which was which.

The damage: two live links in one inbox, and Erik reading the older draft in a tab neither of them knew was open.
Fixed: if that person already has a live negotiation link, sending refreshes it in place and says so — "Erik Lindqvist already had a link to this contract, so that link now shows this version. No second link was created." Signing links are deliberately left out of this, because one signature has to bind one exact copy.

Round 3  The page told her to do two different things at once

With Erik's changes waiting for a decision, the contract page showed a yellow banner saying "Changes returned — 2 rounds awaiting your decision" and, directly underneath it, a blue button saying "Send for review" with the words "Key terms are set — move it into review."

Fixed: when somebody is waiting on you, that is the next step and nothing else is. The bar now reads "Nordfrakt AB is waiting on you — 2 changes to decide" with one button that goes straight there.

Any round  There was no way back to an earlier draft

You could look at any earlier version and compare it with today's. Then you had to retype it. For a small-business owner who has just talked herself into a worse position, "go back to what we sent on Tuesday" is the most obvious button in the world, and it did not exist.

It was worse than that. On a contract negotiated the modern way — clause by clause in the room — the version list said "0 versions" for the whole negotiation. There was nothing there to go back to.

Fixed, both halves. Sending a draft now files a version with a name a person recognises — "Sent to Erik Lindqvist". And every version has a restore link. Restoring saves the wording you have now as its own version first, so nothing is ever destroyed and the history reads honestly: v1 Sent to Erik → v2 Before going back to v1 → v3 Restored from v1. It refuses while a change is still open, and explains why.

Round 6  Erik signed, and his screen still told him to sign

He typed his name into the signature pad, was told plainly that this workspace cannot check his email address and that the record would say so, and pressed Sign anyway. A green box appeared confirming it — a long way down the page, beside the buttons.

Meanwhile the big green band at the very top still read "Ready to sign — read the wording below, then sign or respond on the right." The buttons were correctly spent. The headline was not.

Fixed: the band now says "Erik Lindqvist signed this contract — sent to Wanjiru Kamau at Karibu Catering. There is nothing further for you to do here."

Round 6  Wanjiru was never told he had signed

Erik's signature was filed properly. Her history recorded it. The share row said "Signed". And the top of her contract page carried on saying "Key terms are set — move it into review", with a button offering a step the contract had passed three rounds earlier.

The damage: the only act left in the entire deal was her signature, and no screen mentioned it. She would have found out by opening the audit trail, or not at all.
Fixed: the bar now reads "Erik Lindqvist has signed. Your signature is the only thing left." with a Sign button.

Round 6  The signed copies were never sent — and the reason given was nonsense

When both parties have signed, HaTi emails a sealed copy to each of them. It asked the server to do that a fraction of a second before it had finished saving the fact that the contract was signed. The server looked at the older saved copy, saw a contract that was not signed, and refused with "Contract is not executed yet".

That sentence was then printed in the signature panel of a contract the same panel had just marked Executed and sealed, with both parties listed as Failed.

The damage: nobody received their copy of a signed contract, and the explanation on screen contradicted the panel it was sitting in. This is the same underlying mistake as the lost round — a save that had not finished before something read it back.
Fixed: the save is finished before the copies are requested. Both parties now show Sent. And the "what to do next" bar refreshes the moment the contract is sealed, instead of waiting for a reload.

The four I had listed instead of fixed

After the third cycle I named four problems as the reasons the score was not higher — and then left them there. That was the wrong way round. Three of them were things I had chosen not to fix, not things I could not. Here they are, closed.

Every round  The contract called itself "Drafting" the whole way through

Six rounds of real back-and-forth with a named buyer, and the label at the top still said it was being drafted. The status only ever changed when someone pressed an internal "Send for review" button — which nobody presses once there is a real buyer to send it to.

And fixing it uncovered something worse. The moment the status started moving properly, the page began offering Wanjiru a Sign button on a contract she had sent out ten seconds earlier for Erik to argue with. Inviting someone to sign in the middle of a disagreement is worse than saying nothing at all.

Fixed, both parts: sending it to the other side is what moves it to In Review. And the page now answers whose move it is before it answers anything about signing — "It is with Nordfrakt AB. Nothing needs you until they answer."

Round 6  Five buttons that all sounded like the same thing

Erik's signing page offered Approve & sign, Accept the wording (without signing), Propose edits (redline), Request changes and Decline, side by side as equals. Three of those are the same sentence in a first-timer's head, and every one of them was named after what the software does rather than what the person is doing.

Fixed: the link already knows it is for signing, so there is one button — Sign this contract. Underneath it, one line: "Not ready to sign?". Open it and the other four are there, renamed as things a person does, each with a sentence saying what happens.

All rounds  Erik's page was building the whole negotiation twice

One copy visible, one hidden directly behind it. Both used the same internal names for the same parts — like two identical light switches wired to one bulb. Anything looking for "the send button" found the invisible one, because it came first.

The damage, and it was real: when Erik pressed Send, the word "Sending…" was written onto the invisible copy while the button he was actually looking at said nothing at all. It also cost an hour of this session's own testing, because half my clicks were landing on the copy nobody can see.

The hidden copy was only being kept because an automated test read it. That is a poor reason to ship a trap.

Fixed: there is one copy. The test now reads the copy a person can actually see, which is a better test than the one it replaced. Doing this also caught a second fault: refreshing a link in place had been leaving the reader looking at the previous version.

All rounds  Wanjiru's screen never updated itself

The app checked for news every 45 seconds, whatever she was doing. So she could be sitting on the very contract she had just sent out — the one case where the waiting is the experience — looking at a screen up to three quarters of a minute out of date, with no reason to suspect it.

Fixed, with what was already there: it now checks every 12 seconds while she is looking at a contract that is out with the other side, and immediately whenever she comes back to the browser tab. No new plumbing.
Measured, with her tab never reloaded: Erik sends a proposed change, and her page changes from "It is with Nordfrakt AB" to "Nordfrakt AB is waiting on you — 1 change to decide" 17 seconds later, on its own.

The verdict

9 / 10

Six rounds of real back-and-forth now go through this software without anything falling on the floor. Before this work, three separate things stopped it — and one of them threw away a whole round of a counterparty's work without telling anyone. That is fixed, and checked.

All six rounds work, and the contract is signed, sealed and delivered to both parties at the end. Fifteen faults were found and fixed across four cycles, and every one of them was seen happening on a screen before it was touched and seen working afterwards.

The fourth cycle did not raise the number, and that is deliberate. It closed the four things I had been listing as reasons for the score rather than fixing — which was the wrong way round, and was pointed out. What is left is one reason, and it is the honest one: nobody has looked at any of this on a properly styled screen. A ten means a caterer with no training gets through with zero confusion. I can tell you the steps work. I cannot tell you the screens do, because I have never seen them.

Would a Kenyan SME owner keep using it?

Yes — and now she could recommend it. Before this work she could not have: the negotiation would have started as a signature request, the buyer would have had no way to answer, and one of his rounds would have quietly disappeared. Any of those three would have sent her back to WhatsApp within a week.

What she gets now is the thing the product is actually for: every request in one list, one decision each, the reason attached to the refusal, one link per person, and a way back to Tuesday's draft. That is better than the file called contract-final-FINAL2.docx by a distance.

The single worst friction point that was found

The counterparty's answers were being lost in silence.

Erik accepted a clause and dropped a disputed point. His screen said sent. The server marked it delivered. Wanjiru's contract showed neither, her history had no entry, and because the server believed it had been delivered it never tried again. No error appeared anywhere, on either screen.

It was not a flaw in the negotiation. It was four steps in the wrong order: the answers were held in memory, the screen redrew and pulled the older saved copy back over them, and the save that followed wrote that older copy to disk. Fixed by saving before redrawing, and confirmed on a full run.

What is still not right

One thing, and I cannot fix it from here.

Nobody has seen any of this on a properly styled screen.

The app downloads the file that gives it its colours, spacing and button sizes from the internet. The machine this was tested on has no access to that file, so every screen in all four cycles came up as plain black text on white.

That means I could check what the software does and what it says — and I did, over and over, driving both people through the whole journey in a real browser. It means I could not check whether anything is readable, well spaced, legible on a phone, or big enough to tap. None of the scores above cover that, and none of them should be read as if they do.

Someone needs to open this on a normal computer and look at it. That is the last thing standing between nine and ten.
