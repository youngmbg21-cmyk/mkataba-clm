HaTi — an honest UX review

Two people, one catering contract, six rounds of back-and-forth. Written after reading the code that is actually in the repository today.

How I did this review

I read the real files in the repo — the negotiation room, the contract page, the sharing code, the server routes and the counterparty portal — and then walked two imaginary people through a full negotiation, clicking only the buttons that genuinely exist.

Where a feature is missing, I say it is missing. I have not invented anything. Every claim below points at a file so you can check it.

What reading the code cannot tell me. Some things live on the server as settings, not in the code — the email account is the main one. From the repository I can only see whether the code checks for a setting, never whether your server has it. Where that matters I say so, rather than guessing. This workspace does have working email; that has been tested with a real message to a real inbox.

Out of scope on purpose: the mobile / WhatsApp counterparty experience. You have deliberately parked that, so nothing here asks for it.

The two people

Wanjiru — owner, Karibu Catering, Nairobi

Runs a 14-person catering business. Does her own contracts between kitchen shifts, mostly on a laptop in the office, sometimes on her phone in a matatu. She has never used contract software before. She is fast, impatient, and she trusts paper more than screens. If something takes more than a minute she goes back to WhatsApp and email.

Erik — procurement manager, a Swedish company with a Nairobi office

Buys catering for corporate events. Signs maybe forty supplier contracts a year. He has used DocuSign and a proper CLM at his last job, so he has expectations. He is careful, he reads clauses, and he will not sign anything where he cannot see what changed.

What HaTi genuinely has today

So the rest of the review is fair, here is what really exists. This is a serious piece of software, not a prototype.

  • A contract workspace with a real document editor, clauses that keep durable ids, and a full version history (js/views/contract.js, js/versioning.js).
  • A negotiation room where every proposed change is a tracked item you can accept, reject, comment on or withdraw, and where each change is fingerprinted and chained so tampering shows up (js/negotiation.js).
  • Share links with a purpose — either "negotiate" or "sign" — and a strict list of what data is allowed to leave the building (buildSharePayload in js/core.js).
  • A counterparty portal, a one-time-code login, an internal approval chain, an audit trail, an obligations tracker, a template library, and an AI copilot that can draw charts.
  • Around a thousand automated tests, plus browser checks. The engineering underneath is stronger than the experience on top of it.

Round by round

Step counts are real clicks and keystrokes on the current code, from a standing start on the contract page.

Round 1  Wanjiru sends Erik the first draft

What she wants: get the contract in front of Erik.
Where it happens: js/views/contract.js (the share button) → openShareModal in js/core.js → POST /api/shares in server/server.js.
Steps: 7 on a workspace with email connected, which is what this one is.
  1. Open the contract.
  2. Click Share.
  3. Choose the purpose — negotiate or sign.
  4. Type Erik's name.
  5. Type Erik's email.
  6. Decide whether the link should be durable or one-shot (a checkbox she does not understand).
  7. Create the link. HaTi sends it. This part works.
What works: the send itself. With a mail account configured, the link goes out on its own and Wanjiru never touches copy-and-paste. Seven steps to get a contract in front of someone is reasonable.
Exact point of confusion: step 6. The durable-versus-one-shot checkbox is the only decision on this screen with consequences, and it is the one with no explanation. Durable means the link keeps showing the latest version as she edits; one-shot means it works once and dies. Picking wrong either leaves a live link open longer than she meant, or kills Erik's access halfway through a negotiation. She has no way to know which she is choosing.
Only on a brand-new workspace: until an admin connects a mail account, nothing can be delivered — links, update notices and signing codes all have to be copied out by hand. There is an amber banner on the home page and a warning in this dialog, so the app does say so. The gap is that it is a banner you can ignore forever rather than a step in getting started, and the most serious consequence is not shown where it lands. See item 5 in the list at the end.

Round 2  Erik opens the link and asks for two changes

What he wants: understand what he is looking at, then push back on the cancellation clause and the payment terms.
Where it happens: js/views/portal.js — portalStartOtp (line 1596), portalNegoPhase, the change list and the response post.
Steps: 6.
  1. Click the link in Wanjiru's own email.
  2. Enter the one-time code.
  3. Land on the contract with no explanation of what he is expected to do.
  4. Find the change list.
  5. Write two counter-proposals.
  6. Type his name and send.
What works: the one-time-code login is quick and unfussy. The document keeps its numbering and bullets, so clause 7.2 is still clause 7.2 on his screen.
Exact point of confusion: step 3. He arrives and nothing tells him whether he is being asked to comment, negotiate or sign. The code knows — the share carries a purpose and portalNegoPhase reads it — but the screen never turns that into a sentence like "Wanjiru wants your comments on this draft before anyone signs." He also has to type his own name at step 6, having already proved who he is with a code at step 2. That reads as sloppy to someone who signs forty contracts a year.

Round 3  Wanjiru reads his asks and counter-proposes

What she wants: accept the payment change, reject the cancellation change, and explain why.
Where it happens: the negotiation room — js/views/negotiation.js, backed by negoResolve and negoOpenPoints in js/negotiation.js.
Steps: 5.
  1. Open the negotiation room.
  2. Accept Erik's payment-terms change.
  3. Reject his cancellation change.
  4. Type her reason.
  5. Send the decisions.
What works: this is the strongest screen in the product. One change per row, accept or reject in one click, bulk actions when there are twenty of them, and a running count of what is still open. The change fingerprints mean neither side can quietly rewrite history.
Exact point of confusion: step 4. She types her reason into a comment box, but a comment is a separate thing from the rejection — it goes to the message thread, not onto the change itself. On Erik's side the rejection arrives as a bare "rejected" with his explanation living somewhere else on the page. A rejection without its reason attached is the single most argument-causing thing in a negotiation.

Round 4  Wanjiru edits the wording and sends the new draft

What she wants: rewrite clause 9 as a compromise and get it back to Erik.
Where it happens: the room's "Send to …" goes through openShareModal and creates a new share; the contract page's reshareToLastRecipient refreshes the existing one via PUT /api/shares/:token/payload.
Steps: 4 — but there are two different buttons that both look like "send it to Erik", and they do different things.
  1. Edit clause 9 in the document.
  2. Look for the send button.
  3. Find two of them, on two different screens.
  4. Pick one and hope.
Exact point of confusion: step 3. One path updates the link Erik already has open. The other path mints a new link and leaves the old one behind. Nothing on screen explains the difference. If she picks the wrong one, Erik is reading a stale draft in a tab he never closed and neither of them knows it. This is the kind of thing that ends in "but I sent it to you" on a phone call.

Round 5  Erik is still not happy — a genuine stand-off

What he wants: hold his position on cancellation without killing the deal.
Where it happens: negoAlignment and negoWithdraw in js/negotiation.js; the counterparty bar in js/views/negotiation.js.
Steps: 3 — leave the change contested, or withdraw it.
What works, and works well: HaTi is honest about disagreement. It will not let either side click "Ready to sign" while a change is still contested — the button is greyed out with a line saying exactly what is blocking it. Erik can also withdraw his own ask, which is the polite way to concede and is a verb most contract tools do not have. This is genuinely good design and it is rare.
Exact point of confusion: nothing here is broken, but the room never suggests a way forward. It correctly reports "you are not aligned" and then stops. A first-time user reads a greyed-out button as a fault in the software rather than a fact about the negotiation. One line — "two points still open; talk to Erik or withdraw your ask" — would turn a dead end into an instruction.

Round 6  They agree — and signing goes wrong twice

What they want: sign it and be done.
Where it happens: internalAllSigned in js/approvals.js:103; the signing share in js/core.js; the print block in js/views/portal.js.
Steps: Wanjiru signs, then issues a signing link, then Erik signs — but two things bite.
Blocker one — the order is hidden. internalAllSigned means Wanjiru's side must sign before Erik's signing link becomes live. Nothing tells her that in advance. She sends Erik a signing link, Erik opens it, and it does not let him sign. He emails her. She has no idea why. There is no message anywhere on the screen saying "you need to sign first."
Blocker two — the old link dies silently. Issuing a signing link retires the negotiation link (shareRetiredBySigning). That is correct behaviour, but Erik's open tab just stops working, with no explanation on Wanjiru's screen that it happened.
What works: once they get through it, the executed contract prints properly, with the seal and execution block, and — correctly — contracts that were merely uploaded print exactly as they came in, with no HaTi markings added.

One more thing: versions

Snapshots are now named and saved deliberately, which is a real improvement — the version list reads like a story instead of machine noise. But I searched the whole repository and there is no way to restore a version. No restoreVersion function exists anywhere.

So Wanjiru can look at what the contract said three rounds ago, and she can compare it to now, and then she has to retype it. For a small-business owner who has just talked herself into a worse position, "go back to the draft from Tuesday" is the most obvious button in the world, and it is not there.

The verdict

7 / 10

Strong bones, rough edges. The negotiation model underneath is better than most commercial CLM tools — tracked changes, fingerprints, a withdraw verb, a hard block on signing while people still disagree. Sending works. What lets it down is that the software knows things it never says out loud: which button updates which link, who has to sign first, why a button is greyed out. Every remaining problem in this review is a sentence that should be on the screen and is not.

The single worst friction point

The signing order is a rule in the code that never appears on the screen.

internalAllSigned (js/approvals.js:103) means your side must sign before the other side's signing link goes live. Nothing anywhere tells Wanjiru that. So she does the natural thing — sends Erik the signing link first — and it does not let him sign. He emails her. She has no idea why, because from where she is standing the software simply did not work. This happens at the worst possible moment: after they have finally agreed, when the only thing left is to close. It costs a phone call, and it costs confidence.

The rule itself is right. Only its silence is wrong. Three numbered steps on the signing panel would end it — and the sketch for Round 6 above shows exactly that.

Would a Kenyan SME owner keep using it?

Yes. Wanjiru would keep using HaTi, because the alternative is a WhatsApp thread and three files called contract-final-FINAL2.docx. The negotiation room genuinely solves her problem — she can see every ask in one list, decide each one, and prove afterwards what was agreed. That is worth the learning curve, and the learning curve is not steep.

What she would not do is recommend it yet. The moments that would make her hesitate are all small and all fixable: the failed first signing attempt, the two send buttons, retyping a paragraph because there is no way back to an old version. None of those are reasons to leave. All of them are reasons to say "it's good, but…" — and for a business that grows on word of mouth between caterers, that gap between uses it and recommends it is the whole game.

Eight things to fix, in order

What to doWhy it hurts right nowSize
1. Show the signing order Your side must sign before the other side's link goes live. That rule is in the code and nowhere on the screen, so the first signing attempt fails for a reason nobody can see — at the exact moment the deal is meant to close. Three numbered steps on the signing panel fixes it. Small
2. One send button Two buttons, two behaviours, no explanation. One refreshes the link the other side already has; the other mints a new one and orphans the old. Pick the wrong one and they are reading a stale draft in a tab nobody knows is open. Show one action and one sentence about what it does to their link. Small
3. Attach the reason to the decision A rejection with no reason attached is the biggest cause of a follow-up phone call. The text is already being typed — it just needs to land on the change instead of in a side thread. Small
4. Say what the sharing checkbox does Durable versus one-shot is the only consequential decision on the share screen and the only one with no explanation. Two plain sentences under the box — "keeps showing your latest version" versus "works once, then dies" — remove the guess. Small
5. Warn about unverified signatures where they happen Until a workspace connects a mail account, signatures are recorded as not independently verified, because the code that proves the signer owns their address cannot be sent. The home page banner says this; the signing screen does not. Put it in front of whoever is about to sign, and make connecting mail part of getting started rather than a banner you can ignore forever. Does not affect this workspace — mail is connected here. Medium
6. Tell the counterparty what they are here for The share already carries its purpose. Turn it into one sentence at the top of the portal — "comments wanted" or "ready for your signature" — and stop asking for a name they already proved with a code. Small
7. Add restore-to-version You can look at any past version and compare it, but you cannot go back to one. Retyping a paragraph from three rounds ago is exactly the manual work this product exists to remove. Medium
8. Show link status on the contract Nowhere does it say who currently holds a live link, when they last opened it, or that a link was retired when signing began. The owner is guessing about the one thing she most needs to know. This is also what makes item 2 dangerous. Medium

Nothing above requires new infrastructure. Five of the eight are labels, ordering and wording on screens that already exist; three are small features. None of them touch the mobile / WhatsApp counterparty portal.

One smaller thing did not make the eight, and it is worth doing when you are next in that file: when "Ready to sign" is greyed out because the two sides still disagree, the room says what is blocking it but never what to do about it. One suggested next step — talk to them, or withdraw your ask — would stop a first-time user reading a correct refusal as a broken button.
