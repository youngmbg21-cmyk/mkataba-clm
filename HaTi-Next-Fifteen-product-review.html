i

How these pictures were made

The “today” screens are screenshots of HaTi. The application was started, signed into as an administrator, and loaded with a book of 22 live agreements for a Kenyan FMCG business — Kabras Sugar, Naivas, Bidco, Siginon Freight, Chandaria, Jumia — across all six value streams, with real obligations, expiry dates and requests. Every pixel is the product as built.

The “tomorrow” screens are the same running application with the additions placed into it, using HaTi’s own components: its cards, its bands, its filter controls, its buttons, its status chips. Nothing was redrawn. That is why the two pictures in each pair line up exactly — they are the same page, one second apart.

They are mock-ups made inside the product, not working features. What they prove is placement and weight: where each addition would sit, and how much of the screen it costs.

What looking at the real product changed. The previous edition put the forwarded-contract tray on a row of tabs across the Contracts page. There is no such row — and HaTi already has an Import queue, with its own tile on Home and its own door in the sidebar. So the Mailroom now feeds the queue that exists, which costs nothing at all. Three other placements moved for the same reason, and the contract room was redesigned outright rather than extended — each is marked where it occurs.

Six screens, before and after

Every one of the fifteen lands on one of these six pages. Five of them gain something; the sixth, the contract room, is redesigned. Tick Full size on any picture to see it at the width HaTi actually draws it.

1 of 6

Home

Carries S1 the Mailroom · S6 a notice ready to serve · S8 a lapsed certificate · S9 a watched answer that changed

Home is where a product usually gets fat, and it is the page the fifteen touch most lightly. Two things change, and both are numbers inside furniture that is already on the screen: a Portfolio tile stops reading zero, and one more row appears in a list that already caps itself at three.

The Import queue tile comes aliveS1 — it read “0 docs · nothing waiting” and was greyed out. Forwarded contracts land in the queue HaTi already has, so the tile reads “3 docs · 2 read and ready to confirm” and becomes a door. No new tile, no new page.
A drafted notice leads Prepared for youS6 — a fourth kind of prepared work beside the chase and the renewal memo. The ceiling of three rows is unchanged: it competes for a place rather than adding one, and the sub-line still counts what is on screen.
Two more kinds of alertS8 S9 — a lapsed certificate and a watched answer that changed. Both are rows in the bell’s ranked list, which is the cheapest thing anyone can add to HaTi. The panel does not change shape.
Money owed to us joins the tile catalogueS7 — the four tiles under My work are chosen by each person from a list. One more option on that list; nobody who does not pick it ever sees it.

Weight added: none. No new region, no new tile, no new row type. Every figure here sits in furniture that was already drawn, including on a day when all of it reads zero.

2 of 6

Contracts

Carries S13 bulk amendments · S14 the diligence pack · S8 lapsed documents as a filter

The table is narrowed here the way anybody narrows it — Lifecycle stage: Executed — and the footer says so in the product’s own words: 14 of 22. That is the moment, and only that moment, when “these” means something, so that is when the one new act appears.

“Do this to these 14”S13 S14 — one button, beside the one that is already there, carrying both the amendment campaign and the diligence pack in its menu. It is not drawn at all while the table shows everything, because then it would be an offer to act on the whole book.
A “Required document” filterS8 — a sixth option behind Adapt filters, which is where HaTi already hides the filters most people never use. It draws itself on the bar only while it is narrowing something, exactly as the Signed-year filter does today.
Nothing else moves — same eight columns, same row height, same value-stream legend, same footer arithmetic. The table is the part of HaTi people use most, and it is untouched.
What did not happen hereS1 — the forwarded-contract tray. The first edition put a tab row across this page; there is no tab row, and the Import queue already exists. Reading the real screen removed a whole piece of proposed furniture.

Weight added on an ordinary visit: none. With no filter in force, this page is byte-for-byte what it is today.

3 of 6

The contract room — redesigned, not extended

Carries S2 commercial terms · S8 required documents · S6 serve a notice · S12 the family check

This was the one screen where the additions did not fit, and the honest answer was not a better stacking order. The Key terms tab was already a panel of attributes on the left and a column of unrelated widgets on the right — a decision, a reading, a structure — and adding two more widgets made it card soup. So this screen is redesigned rather than extended, and the design is taken from how serious contract software solves exactly this problem.

What best-in-class does

SAP Fiori — the object page floorplanOne page per object: a header of key facts, then sections and subsections holding forms and tables, reached by an anchor bar. The decisive rule: use a tab bar when the page covers different topics that each have complex content, and sections when the content belongs together and people want to jump within it. SAP design system
Icertis — the Agreement Details pageA top section of mandatory attributes, then attributes grouped under collapsible headings that expand individually or all at once. Labels in uppercase above the value; an attribute with no value shows two dashes. Not a stack of cards — one page of named groups. Icertis help
Sirion — one place per topicTheir clause view was re-laid-out to put the clause, its deviations and its metadata together in one streamlined surface, rather than making the reader assemble them from separate panels. Sirion release notes
Ironclad — hide what is not acted onWorkflow metadata is deliberately hidden from the properties a person edits, because it is not typically amended. Progressive disclosure by usefulness, not by category. Ironclad

So: no new tab. By SAP’s own rule, HaTi’s five room tabs are correct — Key terms, Document, Signing, Obligations and History really are different topics with complex content. A sixth tab for commercial terms would overlap two of them, and HaTi’s own worst fault class is two screens disagreeing about what the product does.

What is wrong is inside the tab. Everything on Key terms belongs to one topic — what this contract says — so by the same rule it should be one page of named sections, not a panel plus a column of cards. That is the Icertis layout, and it happens to be what HaTi’s own Key terms panel is already reaching for: it currently ends with a sentence apologising that the governing law, the liability cap and the payment terms are “in the wording, not in this panel”.

Everything else collapses, and still says something — a shut section is one row carrying its own summary: “A standalone agreement”, “one has lapsed”, “Not written yet”. You usually do not need to open it. This is Ironclad’s rule — hide what is not acted on — and Icertis’s collapsible groups.
Write the brief and the Agreement family both surviveunchanged — as the sections What Copilot read and Related agreements, with their buttons inside. Nothing that exists today is removed; it is renamed and regrouped so there is one place to look instead of five.

One optional rename — the tab now holds more than key terms, so Overview would describe it better. That is a word, not a build, and it is entirely your call.

This is the one screen in the fifteen that gets simpler rather than fuller. Six things to look at become one page with a predictable order; the busiest contract in the book fits without scrolling; and an NDA shows four rows. The contract itself is on the Document tab and does not move by a pixel.

Obligations

Carries S7 what you are owed · S8 certificates as rows

The cheapest change in the whole set, because this page already does every hard part: it groups into bands with a count and a money total, it already carries an amount column, it already tells ours from theirs, and it already chases. Look at the “today” picture — the Side filter is on the bar and the amounts are in the table. Nothing needs inventing.

An “Earned, not claimed” bandS7 — the rebate you crossed the threshold on, the service credits for four late loads, the listing fee for a campaign that was never run. Same band shape, same count at the right, same money total as every other band.
One new verb: ClaimS7 — beside Open, where Chase already sits on the rows below. Money you are owed is a kind of obligation, not a new feature.
The head counts itS7 — “14 outstanding · 4 overdue · KES 5.20M earned, not claimed”. One more fact on a line that already carries two.
Certificates are already rows hereS8 — look at the overdue band in the “today” picture: Product liability insurance evidence, from Kabras Sugar, already overdue. What is missing is the document itself and its expiry, not the row.

Weight added: one band that only exists when something has been earned. The filters, the columns and the row shape are untouched. Left on “Both sides”, the page reads as it does today.

5 of 6

Insights

Carries S10 the exposure register

This is the only piece of new surface in the fifteen, and it is admitted as such: a sixth tab. It earns the space by replacing something worse — those counts exist today inside a weekly document, written as sentences, with no way to press them.

One row per kind of exposureS10 — uncapped liability, open-ended price clauses, indemnities with no ceiling, agreements that renew themselves, lock-ins you cannot exit. A count, the money on paper, and the worst single case.
Every row is a door — See all narrows the Contracts page to those agreements, which is the same door the other Insights tabs already use. Nothing here is a number you cannot open.
The last row is the honest oneS10 — 19 contracts, KES 475.50M, not read closely enough to say anything about. Every competitor hides that figure. It is the sentence that makes the others usable.
No score out of 100 — a made-up rating is worse than a count you can press, and a lawyer cannot defend a number they cannot derive. This is the one place the market standard was deliberately refused.

Weight added: one tab, always there. Together with the Requests columns below, that is the entire cost of the fifteen to somebody who never uses any of them.

6 of 6

Requests

Carries S4 the clock · S5 the tracker link · S3 clearance lanes

Four colleagues have asked for something. Today each request carries a title, a paragraph, who asked, when, the counterparty and the value stream — and three buttons. What it does not carry is the four things that turn an inbox into a queue: how it was routed, who is holding it, when it was promised, and a way for the person who asked to find out without asking again.

Road, With, PromisedS4 — three facts on the right of each row. The road is worked out, not typed: our own template with no money is routine; the other side’s paper with a counterparty nobody has dealt with is a close read.
“Cleared by a lane”S3 — the third request was an NDA on our own template with nothing outside the playbook, so a rule an administrator wrote sent it straight to signature and the clock reads four minutes. The rule itself lives in Settings, where HaTi keeps its rules.
A tracker linkS5 — the way the person who asked finds out where their contract is, without a login and without asking a human. It opens a page outside the app, so it adds nothing to the app’s own surface.
The clock stops when the ball is theirsS4 — and the heading says the median the team actually achieves beside the number waiting. A promised date is a promise the team sets, never a prediction HaTi makes.

Weight added: three facts and a link on a row that had room for them. This and the Exposure tab are the only two changes visible on a day when nothing is happening.

vi

What we did not add

Half of keeping a product light is the list you refused. These are all standard in the category, and each would have cost surface the fifteen above deliberately did not spend.

A fourteenth door in the sidebarEvery one of these could have been a nav item — a Mailroom page, a Notices page, a Compliance page, a Campaigns page. That is how contract systems end up with thirty-one doors and a search box to find them.
A sixth tab in the contract roomCommercial terms and required documents both had a case for it. They are cards instead, in a column built for cards, because a tab is permanent and a card can be absent.
A risk score out of 100Every competitor has one and none can explain it. The exposure register counts instead, and every count opens onto the contracts behind it.
Another dashboardHome answers “what needs me” and Insights answers “what is true”. A third place to look is a third place for the numbers to disagree.
A drag-and-drop workflow builderThe classic enterprise upsell, and the fastest way for a product to lose its opinions. A clearance lane is four conditions and a destination, written down and named on the record when it fires.
AI that signs, or accepts their redline for youHaTi freezes wording at the first signature and records how each signature was proven. Nothing in the fifteen reaches inside that boundary.
Market benchmark data“Your terms versus the market” sells well and cannot be checked. HaTi already has the honest version: what you settled for last time, counted from your own book.
A separate supplier portalHaTi already has a page the other side can open, with careful rules about what may leave the building. A second front door would drift from the first.
Accounting and ERP integrationThe right answer eventually, and not yet. Most of the value in “what you are owed” is knowing what the paper entitles you to, and that needs no integration.

The through-line

Putting the real product beside the proposals changed four of them, deleted a fifth and redesigned a screen. The forwarded-contract tray became a queue that already existed. The Side filter on Obligations turned out to be built. Certificates turned out to be rows the product already draws. And the commercial terms card stopped being a card at all: the Key terms tab needed regrouping, not another box on it. Looking is cheaper than designing.

What is left is fifteen new abilities and two new things to look at — a tab on Insights and three facts on a request. Everything else is a scope, a section, a button, a filter or a row, in furniture that was already on the screen, and none of it appears until it has something to say. One screen ends up simpler than it is today: the contract overview holds two and a half times as many facts in the same height, because it was regrouped rather than filled.
