You asked for three things: a way to send changes to a colleague, a way for them to hold something back, and a rule that stops the send until they have looked. All three are built. Here is what each one does, on screen.

The idea underneath all three

Until now, a change you wrote in the negotiation room was live the moment you saved it. The only thing between a junior's wording and the counterparty's inbox was somebody remembering to ask.

The fix is one idea, and everything else follows from it: written and sent are two different states of the same redline. What you write sits in a working state nobody outside the company can see. Sending is a separate, deliberate act. Everything internal — your boss's note, the wording they killed — happens in the gap between the two, and none of it travels.

HaTi already knew which of your changes the other side had never seen. That set — your unsent asks — is exactly what a review governs. Anything already sent is out of reach: you cannot un-send a redline, so nothing here pretends you can.

One tick per change, and you can untick. Everything starts selected, because that is the common case — but you are usually uneasy about one clause, not all five, and sending the other four with it would stop the rest of the round for no reason. Your reviewer can only rule on what you actually ticked.

Two lists, because a reviewer is answering two different questions: your unsent changes, which they can stop, and the counterparty's open asks, which they can only advise on.

The name field takes typing rather than being a dropdown — a scrolling list stops working at about thirty people. It matches on name or email, and refuses an address with no HaTi account behind it, because only a colleague with a seat can lift a hold.

Where it lands

It appears on your colleague's dashboard, in the card that already holds what needs a person. Reviews lead that card, because they are the only item on it that somebody is personally waiting on — a renewal date does not know your name.

Your verdict

Two vocabularies on purpose. On your changes the reviewer is a gatekeeper — cleared or held, and a hold has a real effect. On the counterparty's, they have no gate to keep, so it is recorded as advice and changes nothing by itself. Calling that "approved" would have been the friendlier word and the more dangerous one.

Handing it back

Hand it back refuses while any of your changes still has no verdict — an unmarked change would otherwise sail through a finished review having been looked at by nobody. Advice on the counterparty's asks stays optional: a reviewer with nothing to add should not have to invent something.

Back on your screen

Step three

What a hold actually does

A held change loses its Send button, wears its badge, and is removed from what travels — on every route, with no setting involved. The send button counts what will really go, and says separately what is being held, because "3 unsent" quietly becoming "2 unsent" reads as a change having vanished.

Before

Both changes would go out together.

After

Only the cleared change travels. The held one stays on your screen, marked, until Achieng lifts it.

The counterparty is never told a review happened, who ruled, or that a hold exists — not the verdict, not the name, not the fact. "We are arguing internally about Clause 6" is worth money to the other side.

Amber is not red, deliberately. Red means refused everywhere else in HaTi, and a change merely sitting with somebody has not been refused — it may well come back cleared. If both were red, the one your reviewer actually stopped would disappear among the ones they simply have not opened yet.

Pressing send while something is still out

Both amber and red stay behind when you send — wording the counterparty reads while your colleague is halfway through it makes their verdict worthless. So the send asks first, and offers the useful third answer rather than only yes and no.

Publish Round, with one change still out

Some of this is still being looked at. 1 change is with Achieng Otieno and has not been answered yet. Sending it now makes the review pointless — their verdict would arrive about wording the counterparty has already read. 2 other changes are ready to go.

The one rule that makes this trustworthy: a hold survives an edit. If you rewrite a change your boss held, it is still held — the card says the wording has moved and asks for another look. The reverse is also true: if you rewrite a change they cleared, the clearance lapses, because they approved words that are no longer there.

Both directions are the same principle. When in doubt, nothing leaves.

Step four

The rule that locks the send

Everything above works on trust. The gate makes it a rule — and it sits in Settings next to the approval rules, because they are the same question asked at two different moments. Approval decides who says yes before signing. This decides who looks before sending.

Settings → next to the approval rules (admin only)

Internal review before redlines go out

Off unless you turn it on. When it is on, changes written here cannot be sent to a counterparty until a colleague has looked at them.

Require an internal review before changes are sent
Every contract Only contracts that deviate from the playbook Only contracts at or above a value

The middle option is the one worth using. Routing everything to the boss is how a review step becomes something people work around; routing the exceptions is how it becomes something they use.

What "locked" means

Three doors send redlines in HaTi — the Share dialog, the round-send onto the counterparty's standing link, and the workbench's own button. All three now ask the same question and print the same sentence. The readiness panel carries it too, as a block rather than a tick-past warning.

Pressing Publish Round while the gate holds

Said before the dialog opens, not after — nobody should fill in an address and a covering note only to be told the wording is still with their boss.

The guardrails

Most of these exist because the feature is worse than useless if any one of them leaks.

RuleWhy
Nobody clears their own wording Only the named reviewer can rule. Anyone who could write a verdict could clear their own change and send it — the same rule the negotiation already applies to accepting proposals.
Viewers cannot be asked A viewer cannot act anywhere else in HaTi either, so a review sent to one would be a request into a void. They are not offered in the list.
A change written after the request is not covered by it Otherwise anyone could slip wording past an open review by writing it a minute late. It shows as unreviewed and blocks the send.
Sent wording cannot be held A hold only ever applies to changes the other side has not seen. Removing something they already hold would read to them as us rewriting what we sent.
The counterparty learns nothing Three separate locks — read-only mounts, the counterparty seat, and their own portal — all refuse to draw any part of a review. Their copy of the contract carries no review data at all.
Everything is on the record The request, every verdict, every note and the hand-back with its tally are written to the contract's audit trail, in the same plain English the rest of it uses.

Somebody who opens a contract on the way to a meeting has to be able to see that their redlines are sitting with their boss, and that one of them is not going out. A screen that stayed silent about a hold would let them believe they had sent something they had not.

What I did not build

Said plainly, so nothing here is a surprise later.

  • No reminders or escalation. A review with a due date that passes is marked overdue on the dashboard and does nothing else — it will not chase your colleague or reassign itself.
  • No verdicts on a phone. Clearing and holding are done on a computer, as above.
  • One review open at a time per contract. Asking a second person means cancelling the first or waiting. Two reviewers ruling on one change at once has no sensible answer, so it is not offered.
  • Email only if the workspace has a mail provider. Without one the review still files and still appears on their dashboard; you are told no email went out.
  • The review panel itself writes no wording — it takes verdicts and notes only. But your reviewer is an ordinary colleague with editing rights, so they can simply open the clause and correct it, and usually that is the better thing for them to do. See the section below.

Can your reviewer change the wording?

The question everybody asks, and the answer is: yes, but not from the review panel.

The review takes verdicts and notes — cleared, held, advice, a sentence saying why. It writes no wording. But the person reviewing is a colleague with ordinary editing rights, so nothing stops them opening the clause and fixing it, and for a senior reviewer that is usually better than writing "make it 24 months" and waiting a round.

When they do, the change keeps its number and its author — the ask is still yours, they changed the words — your original wording stays recoverable in the history, and the card says so:

Their own verdict goes stale when they edit, so a hold they placed survives their own rewrite until they lift it. And the line disappears if you take the wording back — it is a statement about who wrote what is there now.

The counterparty is told none of this. They receive the corrected wording with no hint that two people touched it.

Trying it

  1. Check out the branch and open the app as you normally would.
  2. Open a contract, go to Negotiate, and propose a couple of changes.
  3. Press Internal review in the toolbar and send it to a colleague.
  4. Sign in as that colleague — their dashboard shows it. Clear one change, hold the other, hand it back.
  5. Back on your own account, press Publish Round and watch the held one stay behind.
  6. To see the gate, turn it on in Settings and try to send before asking anyone.
